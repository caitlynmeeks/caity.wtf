Episode 16

The Mystery of the Helpful Flood

Icod de los Vinos, the plaza below the Drago. The eve of San Andrés, November 2041. Also Acornmouth Bay, where it is always raining. Both at once ⁓ that is the trick of this one.

Welcome to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Maisie Ardilla, P.I. Wipe your feet.

The rain in Acornmouth came down like it was gettin' paid by the inch, which suited me fine. Rain's honest. Rain falls down. You can set your hat by a thing like that.

That was before the creek started runnin' backwards.

It was a Tuesday when the frog came up my stairs again. Second time in one career, which for a frog is sayin' something. Last time, somebody drained his pond, and I got his water back, and we had both figured that closed the book on water as a subject. Now he stood in my doorway drippin' ⁓ and here's the part that put my tail up ⁓ drippin' fresh. Pond-fresh. Not rain.

"Maisie," he said. "It's back."

"The water?"

"All of it. And then some. From the wrong direction."

I put on the hat. I took the magnifying glass, which I don't need, but a detective without a magnifying glass is just a nosy squirrel, and a town can't put its faith in that. A pond's got exactly two ways of goin' wrong: empty and extra. I'd seen empty. Empty has a villain shaped like a cravat, and that villain was sulkin' in a knothole estate on the east side and hadn't glided anywhere in months ⁓ I checked, I always check. Extra, though.

Extra was new.

❦

Behind the curtain, in the fog country, Saga clipped her script to its stand and looked out through the peephole at a plaza that smelled of chestnuts and new wine.

Icod had filled up slow and happy since noon. It was the eve of San Andrés, which in Icod means three things arrive at once: the year's malvasía comes up from the cellars, the castañas go onto the braziers by the sackful, and every boy in town spends the day carrying a waxed wooden board around under his arm and looking at the steep streets the way surfers look at the sea. The market stalls ran the whole east side of the plaza. The chestnut smoke lay over everything, sweet and woody, the smell of the north being generous with itself.

And above the rooftops to the west stood the Drago ⁓ the thousand-year dragon tree, older than the town, older than the island's Spanish name, a green thunderhead balanced on a trunk like braided stone. It had presided over roughly a thousand of these eves. It presided over this one the same way: without comment.

An hour earlier the plaza had roared ¡CHACHO! at the sky, twice, loud enough to rattle the bunting ⁓ Los Magníficos closing the afternoon on the wire while Ruymán whistled them up and half of Icod whistled back. Now the wire was down, the light was going amber, and the folding stage stood in the middle of it all with its curtain shut and its secrets in order.

Mostly in order.

"They will not hold still," Auxi said.

She was eleven now, and this was her night: her first show on tadpoles. Ten green felt finger puppets sat on their rack in two rows of five, googly eyes pointing every direction the way googly eyes do, and Auxi checked their order a fourth time, and Saga, who knew precisely what a fourth check was about, did not say anything about it.

"They're tadpoles," Saga said instead. "Holding still would be out of character."

"Greta keeps leaning."

"Greta always leans. That's the part. You're not holding puppets, you're holding a family ⁓ the leaning is the whole point of Greta."

Auxi looked at the big tadpole with the skeptical respect of a girl being handed a legacy. Jef went past behind them with the foghorn bottle under one arm and the fog spool under the other, walking the particular walk of a fourteen-year-old who is load-bearing and knows it. Gertie sat on the crate by the prop table with Maisie Ardilla on her right arm, running the puppet's little paws through a warm-up she would have denied was a ritual, and the puppet worked an invisible magnifying glass, and checked an invisible hat, and cracked its felt knuckles.

"Full house," Mateo said, appearing from the stage's far side with his synth already humming on the trolley. He said it the way other men say calm seas. Then he tipped his head back toward the west end of the plaza, where two very large men from Gran Canaria were coiling the last of the wire rig, and said something to Saga in the shorthand of a man who has been watching people his whole life: "Keep half an eye on the brothers tonight, mija. Somebody offered them a boat."

❦

An hour before curtain, Saga walked Auxi up past the church to return an extension cable to the sacristan, who had lent it on the condition that it come back before the saint needed it, and that was how they came upon the terrero.

Icod keeps its sand ring behind the church wall, and on San Andrés eve the ring was working: the luchada, the exhibition bouts, two villages' worth of people around a circle of raked sand under strung bulbs, and in the middle of it, rolling up his trouser legs with great deliberation, somebody Saga knew.

"That's Tomás," she said. "From Gertie's yard."

Tomás of El Fraile, sixty-two, metalsmith, had come north on the Guagua with a crate of forge work for the fiesta, and had been recognized within the hour ⁓ because the island keeps its luchadores the way it keeps its saints' days ⁓ and there had never been any possibility of him leaving Icod without an agarrada. The boy across the sand from him was the town's young champion, built like a doorway, and visibly working out how to be respectful and win at the same time, which was going to cost him.

They took their grips on each other's rolled shorts. They went still. The stillness went on ⁓ lucha is mostly stillness, the way chess is mostly sitting ⁓ and then something happened too economical to fully see, a shift of hip and a borrowing of the boy's own strength, and the doorway came off his feet, turned once in the strung-bulb light, and was set down on his back in the sand the way you set down something you intend to keep. Tomás's hand was out before the sand finished moving. He pulled the boy up. He patted his shoulder clean. The applause, long and warm, was not for the winning.

"You put him down soft," Saga said, when he came over to the rope.

"There is no other way to put a person down," Tomás said, and went to find his crate.

On the way back, in the lane below the church, five Icod kids had a football going ⁓ head to knee to head, around the circle, counting in shouts, showing off for everyone the fiesta funneled past. At thirty-one a header went long, and the ball came bouncing down the cobbles and rolled itself flat against Saga's boot.

The circle looked at her. The lane went quiet the way a plaza goes quiet for a wire act.

Saga picked it up and ⁓ because there are laws, and everyone her age keeps them ⁓ threw it back in properly: two hands, from behind the head, both feet planted, the full ceremony. The tallest kid took it down on one knee without letting it touch the ground, and the count started again from zero, and Auxi, walking backward so as not to miss any of it, said: "Thirty-one is nothing. In Callao we go to fifty."

This was not true, and was exactly the right thing to say.

I followed the creek up out of town, and the creek got less and less creek as I went, till it wasn't a creek at all. It was a lake with paperwork. Acornmouth Creek had run through that cut since before anybody's grandmother was anybody's grandmother, and now it sat there, wide and flat and smug, backin' up into the alder roots, and at the head of it stood the biggest piece of construction I ever saw that nobody asked for.

A dam. And not some pile of sticks, neither. This thing had courses. It had a spillway. It had, so help me, a decorative element.

It also had a plaque. Brass, polished, bolted on at readin' height, which told me the builder expected visitors and expected 'em grateful. The plaque said:

CREEK IMPROVEMENT No. 1
You're welcome.
⁓ C.Q.D.

I stood in the mud with the rain runnin' off my hat, and I got that feelin' I get. The one in the tail. Because a crook hides, see. A crook covers his tracks, wears gloves, gets an alibi and a haircut. Whoever did this had done the opposite of hidin'. Whoever did this had signed it, and shined the signature.

And that scared me worse than Glidewell ever did. You can fight a man who knows he's wrong. What walked around Acornmouth now was somethin' else: a party of one who thought he was the good news.

Back in town it was everywhere, once I had the eye for it. The evidence room at the station: alphabetized, which sounds fine till you learn the sergeant had a system, and the system was his, and now he couldn't find a thing includin' his own chair. The foghorn: reschedulled ⁓ rescheduled ⁓ to blow at "optimal intervals," which meant it now went off when no ships were comin' and stayed quiet when they were. Madge the millipede had come out of her sixth year of quitting to find her desk "consolidated." She was forty pages into a fresh resignation and writin' with all her hands.

"Who," I asked her, "is C.Q.D.?"

Madge looked at me over her glasses, all hundred of her knuckles goin' white on the pen.

"He introduced himself," she said. "He has a card."

The show began, as La Tropa shows begin, by not beginning ⁓ Mateo's synth laying the sound of the plaza's own afternoon back under the plaza's evening, the chestnut smoke, somebody's laugh, the bell of San Marcos ringing once for no reason the bell ever explained. The crowd went from talking to listening the way a tide changes, without a single announcement anywhere in it.

The curtain opened on Acornmouth. Jef cranked the fog spool, and gray muslin rolled across the little bay, and the foghorn bottle moaned, and three hundred people in a plaza that smelled of roasting chestnuts leaned in, delighted, toward a rainy imaginary town.

And then the island decided to help.

The fog of the fog country came down off the mountain the way it does on a November evening in Icod ⁓ not drifting, arriving, a soft white authority rolling up the streets from the direction of the sea, and it slid into the plaza and over the stalls and through the lantern light, and it upstaged Jef flat. The audience gasped at exactly the wrong moment for exactly the right reason. On stage, Maisie Ardilla stood in muslin fog up to her felt knees while real fog curled around the stage's legs, and Saga heard Jef, behind the scenery, whisper the whisper of a stagehand being outdone by an island: "Oh, come on."

"Leave it in," Saga whispered back. "It's working for us now."

It was somewhere in that first white half hour, while Maisie interviewed a millipede with a grievance, that Saga heard the Spanish start up, low, off the stage's west corner ⁓ Mateo and the brothers, fast, the fast Spanish that went by her formal classroom ear like water over stones. She caught maybe one word in four. She caught Garachico. She caught el viento ⁓ the wind ⁓ twice. She caught Ayoze laughing in the way people laugh when a thing is not funny, and Echedey not laughing at all, and her father's voice going down into the low register he used for things that mattered, the one that made even the synth sound like it was leaning in.

She had a show to run. She ran it. But she filed the corner of her eye where her father kept his, and she noticed, through the peephole, that the brothers had stopped coiling the wire, and stood together in the real fog looking out through the gap in the rooftops toward the sea ⁓ east along the coast, where on the clearest day of a very good year, which this was not, which it almost never was, a person standing high in Icod can see past the long dark shoulder of Anaga to a shadow on the horizon that is not a cloud.

Gran Canaria. One channel and one closed world away. They looked anyway. The fog did not offer it, and they looked anyway, the way you check a pocket you know is empty.

❦

I found him at the creek at dawn, up on his own dam, walkin' the top course with a clipboard.

Cornelius Q. Dampwood. A beaver. Big fella, glossy, teeth like a matched set of chisels, and wearin' the one thing no crook in my whole career ever wore, which was a high-visibility vest. He'd made it himself. He was proud of it. He was proud of everything, that was the thing about him you got in the first four seconds ⁓ pride came off him like heat off a stove, and none of it, not one degree, was the guilty kind.

"You must be the detective!" he said, and came down the dam face like it was a staircase, which, I noticed, he had built it to be. "Wonderful. I hoped the town would send someone. Feedback is a gift."

"Mister Dampwood. That your work up there?"

"Creek Improvement Number One." He said it the way a mother says a baby's name. "Flow regulation, seasonal storage, and a decorative element. Before me, do you know what this creek was doing?"

"Runnin' through town. Like folks liked."

"Meandering," he said, with the pity of a surgeon describin' a bad knee. "Losin' twelve percent to seepage. Floodin' nobody's schedule but its own. A creek, Detective, is just a river that nobody ever managed."

I have questioned every kind of animal there is. I have had lies told to me in eleven accents, and twice in writing. I stood in front of this beaver with my magnifying glass in my hand and I understood I was somewhere new, because every word out of him was true, and every fact was documented, and the whole thing together was wrong the way a wrong note is wrong, which no amount of documentation ever fixed.

"The frog's pond is underwater," I said.

"Improved," said Dampwood. "It was a pond. Now it's a reservoir." He flipped the clipboard around to face me. There was a form on it. The form said FEEDBACK, and under that it said How helpful was your improvement today?, and under that there were five little acorns for me to circle.

I looked at that form a long time, in the rain.

"Mister Dampwood," I said, "in my line of work I meet two kinds of trouble. Trouble that runs when I show up, and trouble that don't. You're a third kind. You're trouble that offers me a pen."

He beamed like I'd given him all five acorns. "I do try," he said.

❦

"He is acapallo," Gertie whispered, behind the curtain, with Maisie Ardilla resting on her knee between scenes like a boxer between rounds. "The beaver. I used it right this time, yes?"

"No," Saga whispered back.

"Ha!" said Gertie, delighted, as if wrongness at this word were a streak she was maintaining on purpose. Which, Saga had begun to suspect around the third month of this, it was.

Out front, Mateo's synth walked the story up the creek. Backstage, Auxi stood at her rack with two tadpoles already on her fingers and eight in reserve, and her lips were moving. Saga leaned over. Auxi was rehearsing the leaning ⁓ tipping Greta a few degrees, correcting, tipping. Serious as a bomb-disposal course.

"They come on after the reservoir scene," Saga said. "You'll hear the frog say my kids are swimmin' in a filing system. That's you."

"That's me," Auxi agreed, in the voice of someone confirming coordinates.

Past her, at the west corner, the rapid Spanish had stopped. Mateo was back at the synth. The brothers stood where the wire rig lay coiled at their feet like something asleep, and Ayoze had his arms crossed, and Echedey had his hands in his pockets, and neither of them was looking at the other, which for two men who finished each other's sentences was its own kind of loud.

The town meeting was standin' room only, which in Acornmouth means the hall was full and the fog was waitin' its turn outside.

One by one they got up. The sergeant, who couldn't find his chair. The lighthouse keeper, whose foghorn now performed at times of statistical elegance and no earthly use. The frog, whose ten kids were doin' laps of a filing system. Madge read out selections from the resignation, which had reached sixty pages and developed chapters. And in the front row, takin' notes with his own pencil in his own notebook, nodding at every complaint like it was applause, sat Cornelius Q. Dampwood, glowin'. "Wonderful engagement," he said, at intervals. "This is exactly the conversation we should be having."

The Mayor stood on both legs and did what the Mayor does, which is nothing, at length.

And I sat in the back with my hat down, doin' the arithmetic. You can't run a beaver like him out of town ⁓ he'd thank you for the exercise. You can't arrest a fella whose every crime is an improvement with the receipts attached. And you can't shame a creature that keeps its whole supply of shame in a jar marked other people's.

But there's one thing a Dampwood can't survive, and I'd been holdin' it in my paw since the creek.

The feedback form.

I found him after the meetin', by the notice board, where he was straightenin' the notices. I took out his form and I took out my pencil and I circled one acorn ⁓ one, out of five ⁓ and I handed it back and watched all the glow go out of him like somebody dammed it.

"One?" he said. His teeth clicked. "One?"

"One," I said. "And you earned that acorn, don't get me wrong. Prettiest dam I ever saw. On the wrong water." I let that sit. Then I leaned in, easy, the way you set a hook you already know the fish wants. "The Hollow floods every winter, Mister Dampwood. East side of town. Storm water comes down that ravine every December and drowns forty root cellars, regular as church, and the whole of Acornmouth has just learned to keep its acorns wet. Now that's a water problem. That one's got teeth." I put my pencil away. "But I expect it's beyond improvin'. Bein' honest, I don't think there's a beaver alive that could manage that ravine."

You want to know what it sounds like when a time-and-motion beaver hears the words beyond improving?

It sounds like a clipboard hittin' the mud.

The tadpoles went on at the reservoir scene and Auxi was flawless.

Better than flawless: Greta leaned. Leaned characterfully, leaned like a big sister who has decided the family crisis is somebody else's turn, and the plaza laughed low and warm, and behind the rack Saga watched Auxi's shoulders come down from around her ears mid-scene ⁓ the exact moment a job stops being a test and starts being a joy, visible from behind, in the shoulder blades of an eleven-year-old.

Then, from the front rows, solemn as an ambassador, a boy of about seven approached the stage. The show kept going above him. He waited at the stage's edge with a thing in both hands until Maisie Ardilla, between lines, glanced down ⁓ and he placed one roasted chestnut, still warm from the brazier, on the boards at the puppet's felt feet, and retreated without a word.

The first case. The Mystery of the Missing Chestnut. Somebody's father had told him. Maisie looked at the chestnut, and looked at the boy, and did the only correct thing, which Gertie found in the wrist without being told: the puppet picked the chestnut up, weighed it in both paws, bit it once to check it was genuine ⁓ the plaza roared ⁓ and touched the brim of the too-big hat.

Evidence, said Maisie Ardilla, and pocketed it.

Saga, behind the curtain, wrote nothing down, and kept it forever anyway.

The dam was gone by Thursday.

Not broken ⁓ relocated. Course by course, spillway and all, moved overnight and overnight and overnight by one beaver working like a whole public works department, up out of the creek cut and around the shoulder of the hill to the mouth of the Hollow, where it sat by the first of December lookin' like it had grown there, with the decorative element facin' town.

The creek came back down its old bed shy and muddy, like a cat let back in the house. The frog's pond emptied to pond again. The tadpoles surveyed their restored kingdom and found it two sizes smaller than the filing system and complained, because they were kids, and that's the job.

And when the December storm came down the ravine ⁓ and it came, mean as always, three days of it ⁓ the Hollow held. First winter in living memory that Acornmouth kept its root cellars dry, and the town walked up in the rain to see it, and stood around the dam in a big quiet circle the way folks stand around a thing that has just saved them a season's food.

Somebody started it. Don't matter who. Madge says it was the frog, the frog says it was Madge. Somebody handed Cornelius Q. Dampwood a feedback form ⁓ his own form, the acorns and all ⁓ with all five acorns circled, and then the next one, and the next, till the beaver stood on his own spillway holdin' forty forms to his chest in the rain, and his teeth were clickin', and it wasn't the cold.

"Feedback," he said, when he could talk, "is a gift."

Case closed. Nobody run out of town, nobody in cuffs, nothin' destroyed ⁓ just one busy creature aimed, at last, at the right water. In my line you don't get many endings where the whole town wins and the villain does too. When you get one, you take your hat off. I took my hat off.

It was rainin', of course. Rain's honest. It falls straight down.

"And that's our show!"

And the imaginary rain went back to being a watering can, and the muslin fog rolled backwards onto its spool with its squeaky crank ⁓ and the island, with a courtesy nobody could have staged, chose those same minutes to take its own fog back up the mountain, so that the two fogs left together, the false one and the true one, and Icod came out from underneath into a clear cold November night full of lantern light and chestnut smoke and applause.

The market broke up warm and slow. The malvasía did what new wine does to a plaza's volume. Somewhere on the steep streets above, the tablas boys were already running ⁓ you heard them before you saw them, wooden boards on wet stone, a sound like enormous zippers, followed by the shrieks of the deathless ⁓ practicing for the night when the whole town would slide downhill on purpose, because it was San Andrés, and that is a thing Icod does and has always done and could not entirely explain to you.

Auxi was retelling Greta's lean to her mother with both hands. Jef was coiling muslin and explaining to anyone in range that the real fog had been his idea, basically. Gertie sat Maisie Ardilla on the edge of the folded stage, facing the plaza, one warm chestnut still in the puppet's pocket.

Saga found her father breaking down the synth.

"The boat," she said. "What did they tell him?"

"No."

She looked across at the brothers, who were loading the wire rig and arguing about whether a sentence had been finished correctly, in the manner of men who had recovered themselves. "The wind is wrong," Mateo added, in the voice of a man quoting.

"The wind is fine," Saga said.

"Sí." Her father closed the synth case, and looked where the brothers were not looking, north and east, past rooftops and fog and one whole channel of dark water. "It is always fine. And it is always wrong."

Above the town, uphill of the lanterns, the Drago stood against the stars with its thousand rings of island inside it. It had watched the Guanches name the mountain. It had watched the ships come, and the ships stop coming. It had watched three hundred people laugh at a beaver in a vest, and one boy give a squirrel a chestnut, and two big men decline a boat toward everything they missed. The Drago said nothing.

It was extremely good at this.

A few words for the curious

The north of the island, this time, on its biggest night.

  • San Andrés and las tablas: on the eve of Saint Andrew, Icod de los Vinos opens the year's new wine ⁓ and its boys wax wooden boards and hurl themselves down the steepest streets in town, a tradition nobody can fully explain and nobody would dream of stopping. Brakes are a philosophy. The quiet changed none of this.
  • Castañas: chestnuts ⁓ the north slopes grow them, the braziers roast them, and the whole plaza smells like the island being generous with itself. Maisie's first case involved one. Icod remembers.
  • Malvasía: the wine that made Icod famous four centuries before anyone reading this was born. Shakespeare's characters drink it under the name malmsey. The cellars never closed.
  • The luchada: San Andrés brings out Icod's terrero ⁓ the raked sand ring of lucha canaria, the islands' own wrestling: grip, stillness, put your opponent down without humiliating him, and help him up after. Tomás of El Fraile, installment six's metalsmith, was one in his youth. The island keeps its luchadores the way it keeps its saints' days.
  • El Drago: the Drago Milenario, the great dragon tree of Icod ⁓ a thousand years old give or take, older than the island's Spanish name, still standing in 2041, on schedule to outlast every argument ever held beneath it. In this episode it watches. It is extremely good at this.
  • Acapallo: the La Charla word from installment six ⁓ a person who is annoying in a specific way that they do not realize they are being annoying. Saga maintains Gertie still uses it wrong. Gertie maintains her streak. Cornelius Q. Dampwood, of Acornmouth, is the term's new textbook illustration ⁓ used correctly, for once, by everyone.
