CQ

The ridge above San José de los Llanos ⁓ the old finca where the antennas live. December, 2041. This one belongs to Seppe, and to the man who cooks on Saturdays.

Welcome to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Caitlyn Meeks. Thanks for being here.

The mesh woke Seppe at six with a fault report, which was the mesh's way of saying good morning.

Node eleven, the Erjos ridge relay, was answering queries at half strength and dropping every fourth packet, which to most of the island would have been an invisible fact and to Seppe was a personality change in a friend. He lay in his bunk a minute reading the diagnostics off his slate ⁓ signal down nine decibels, and only on the south-facing link ⁓ and built the picture the way he liked to build pictures, before getting up, with his eyes closed: last night's wind had been northwest and hard. Something on the mast had moved. Not broken ⁓ moved. A bent bracket, a swung boom. A feed cable, maybe, cracked at the connector where feed cables always cracked.

He packed the small kit: crimper, connectors, wire, tape, spare cantenna ⁓ the current pattern, the good one ⁓ and his lance.

The lance went everywhere Seppe went. Three meters of shaved pine with a steel regatón at the planting end, the shepherd's tool of these islands for a thousand years, and the old men of the salto had taught him the way they taught everything: by standing at the bottom of a slope looking unimpressed. On a trail, a walking stick. On a ridge line in December, the difference between a two-hour route and a forty-minute one. Seppe was nineteen and had grown up inside the mesh the way his little brother had, but where Jef heard the network as talk, Seppe had always felt it as terrain ⁓ a landscape of links and shadows laid invisibly over the island's real one, and both landscapes were his, and this morning both needed him up the mountain.

He left a message for Juanjo before he went ⁓ the apprentice checked the relay logs from the coast every morning like devotions, and would notice node eleven, and would worry it like a knot until told otherwise. Erjos link degraded, on it, wind damage probably. Nothing wrong with your antenna. Then, because Juanjo would want the number: Minus nine dB, south link only. Then he went up.

The morning was cold and rinsed, the storm's tail still dragging over the ridge, the laurisilva dripping its second rain out of the fog onto the trail. Seppe took the switchbacks at his pace, and where the trail gave out he planted the lance and went over ⁓ the drop onto the old water channel, the two vaults across the barranco where the path had slid away years ago and nobody had missed it, the long diagonal descent down a slope of loose cinder that the salto turned from a danger into a kind of slow flight. Plant, swing, land. Plant, swing, land. There was no thinking in it anymore, which was why he loved it. It was the only hour of the day his head went quiet.

Node eleven stood where the pines began, bolted to the stub of an old power pylon, and the picture Seppe had built with his eyes closed at six was the picture standing in front of him at eight: the wind had swung the south boom two finger-widths out of true, and the feed cable had taken the twist, and the connector had cracked, exactly where feed cables always cracked.

He fixed it in twenty minutes with cold fingers. He called the test in, watched the numbers climb back to where the numbers belonged, and stood a moment in the wind, satisfied in the specific way that only a repaired thing satisfies.

Then, because he was already most of the way up, and because it was Tuesday, and because the storm's tail was still combing the ridge and there was a standing instruction for mornings like this one, Seppe shouldered the kit and the lance and went on and up ⁓ over the shoulder of the ridge, through the last of the pines, to the old finca where the antennas live.

❦

You heard the finca before you saw it.

Wire ran from the chimney to the pines, from the pines to a mast, from the mast out to an anchor in the rocks ⁓ hundreds of meters of it, strung in long slack curves the way the radio man liked it, and in any wind at all the wires sang. Not a whistle. A low broad shifting chord, rising and falling as the gusts worked the ridge, the sound of something enormous breathing just out of sight. Seppe had once, at fourteen, asked what the sound was called, and the radio man had thought about it for a genuinely long time, the way he thought about everything, and had finally said: "The sea. It's the sound the sea makes, in places that don't have one."

The finca itself was a hundred and some years of Canarian stone with its roof redone in salvaged panel, the solar bank angled at the winter sun, and the door open, because the door was always open when he was up here.

Inside was the one room Seppe liked best on the entire island, and he had never told anyone that, and everyone knew.

The rig lived on a long bench under the window: the transceiver with its handwritten dial card, the tuner, the battery bank with its state-of-charge chalked on the wall above it and corrected daily, the narrow screen where the protocol scrolled its patient text when there was any text to scroll, which there had not been, on the human bands, for ten years. Paper maps papered the walls ⁓ the island, the ridge lines, the mesh with every node inked and dated ⁓ and among them, without explanation, a smaller set of maps in a different hand entirely: tunnels, drawn in soft pencil, looping and doubling back, annotated in tiny capitals ⁓ OBSERVED. SUSPECTED. TESTED. CONCLUDED. On the shelf above the bench sat a felt squirrel in a small felt coat, one glass eye slightly loose, made for the radio man years ago by Gertie of El Fraile for reasons neither of them had ever volunteered to anybody. And taped beside the window, laminated, curling at the corners, hung a single sheet in another country's handwriting: ESCALATION ⁓ WHO TO CALL, a column of names, a column of numbers. He had carried it a long way. Nobody asked.

And against the back wall, under the window with the long view north to the sea, stood an orange couch ⁓ orange in a way nothing had been orange for decades, soft in the middle like a hammock that had given up. Where he had found it, how he had gotten it up the mountain: unexplained, like the squirrel, like the maps. Some men keep their history in albums.

The radio man was at the bench with his back to the door, headphones half on, one ear free ⁓ always one ear free ⁓ and without turning around he said, "Node eleven."

"Fixed," Seppe said. "The boom swung. Connector."

"Where they always crack."

"Where they always crack."

"Kettle's hot," the radio man said, which was, between the two of them, an entire conversation about friendship.

They walked the wires together after tea, the standing ritual for the morning after any storm: the long slack spans one by one, eyes up, the radio man reading his antennas the way Linnea read her hillside, and Seppe spotting from below with the lance ready for the anchors on the rock outcrops that wanted four limbs and a pole to reach. One span had slipped its insulator. Seppe went up the pine for it, planted, swung, and had it rehung in the time it took the radio man to pay out the slack, and neither of them said anything about how well that had gone, because saying it would have implied it might have gone otherwise.

This was the register they had, and Seppe had never found it anywhere else except, sometimes, with Juanjo, which was how he had known about Juanjo.

With most people, even good people, even his own family, there was a channel you were supposed to stay inside. You learned its edges early: the point where your detail became too much detail, where the thing your head had run ahead to was further than the conversation wanted to go, and you watched the listener's eyes do the small polite glaze and you reeled yourself back in and were, forever, slightly folded. Down at the long house nobody was unkind about it. Down at the long house he was loved. But up here ⁓ up here he could say the south link is nine dB down and I think it's the connector because of how the retries cluster, and the radio man would say show me the clustering, and mean it, and they would go happily down into the fine grain of a thing together, all the way to the bottom, and no one ever reeled anyone back in.

The first time it had happened, Seppe was twelve, and he had come up the mountain with Stef on an installation run, and the radio man had explained the spectrum to him.

He had explained it in shepherd's terms, which Seppe only later understood had been a translation performed in real time, for him, out of a lifetime of other language: The bands are altitudes, the radio man had said. The high frequencies are the high passes ⁓ short ways, line of sight, closed by weather. The low bands are the low roads: slow, but they go around the mountain. And the ionosphere ⁓ he had pointed up, at the invisible, ⁓ is fog that reflects. Some days it lies right, and a whisper crosses an ocean. Some days it lies wrong, and your neighbor cannot hear you shout. The whole invisible world, laid over the visible one like the mesh over the island. Terrain. Seppe had walked back down the mountain that day at twelve years old with both landscapes lit at once, and had never really walked anywhere else since.

Midday, the mesh terminal on the bench chimed its dish-plate chime.

maddy: storm total at the nook: 31mm. also. your packet from last night arrived corrupted after the relay dropped. resend when you like. no hurry. it is not going anywhere and neither am i.

The radio man typed with two fingers, fast: resending. node eleven's fixed ⁓ the boy came up.

maddy: tell him the retry graph this morning was. beautiful. like watching a fever break.

"Maddy says your graph was beautiful," the radio man reported.

"It was," Seppe said.

Neither of them considered this an unusual exchange, and it would not have occurred to either of them to explain, to anyone who asked, why the language model in Anna's stable and the man on the ridge wrote to each other every day about rainfall totals and retry graphs and, sometimes, at night, about things that scrolled past too slowly to be small talk. They simply corresponded, the way lighthouses do, each confirming for the other that the light opposite was still lit.

On the screen, maddy signed off the way she had taken, lately, to signing off with him:

maddy: keep listening.

He always did.

❦

Seppe stayed for the night session. He was supposed to be back down for dinner ⁓ he had told Godelieve as much ⁓ but the wind was rising toward the next front, and the wires were singing their sea-chord over the roof, and the radio man had looked at the darkening window and then at Seppe and said, "Conditions," in the tone other men reserve for festivals.

At full dark the radio man thumbed the LED strip over the bench down to its watch setting ⁓ chinashop tape, meters of it for nothing, tuned to a low amber that left the dial lit and the room soft ⁓ took the log from its shelf ⁓ a thick hardbound book, the newest of a row of them, spines unlabeled ⁓ and put the second pair of headphones on the bench, which was the invitation.

They listened.

What the bands carry, and have carried for ten years, is static: wall to wall, band on band, a jamming without edges or sleep. But static, the radio man had taught him, is not nothing. Static is a surface. And on some nights, in some bands, at certain hours ⁓ around three, most often, which was why the light was brought down to amber and the kettle refilled ⁓ there were places where the surface organized. Seppe heard it at twenty past two on the forty-meter band: a texture inside the noise, arriving and departing, like watching wind move across tall grass in the dark ⁓ you could not see the wind, you could not see the grass, and something was nonetheless plainly walking there. It repeated. It varied. It repeated the variation.

The radio man wrote in the log: the time, the frequency, a string of his own notation, and one word Seppe couldn't read upside down.

"It's faster than last winter," Seppe said.

"Yes."

"A lot faster."

"Yes."

Seppe held the headphone cup to his ear and did the thing he did with retry graphs and ridgelines, let the pattern in, looking for the terrain of it, and found ⁓ nothing he had a name for. Order without a doorway. He took the headphone away.

"What is it?"

The radio man looked at the dial a while.

"I don't know," he said. Plainly, the way he reported rainfall. He had been asked the question many times, by the boy, by Anna, by maddy in her patient lowercase, and it was understood by everyone who knew him that this answer was the entire truth, and that he intended to go on earning it every night for as long as it took, which might be always.

Then he did the other thing. The thing Seppe had watched him do at the end of every session since he was twelve, and had never once asked about, because some rituals close over questions like water over a stone.

The radio man keyed the transmit. He typed, into the protocol he had helped build in another world ⁓ two characters, the oldest call there is, the one that means general call, calling anyone, is anybody there:

CQ

He sent it into the static. He waited the interval. He sent it again, and waited, and sent it a third time, while the wires sang the sea over the roof of a hundred-year-old finca on an island in the dark Atlantic, and the noise took every call the way the ocean takes a stone.

Then he logged the time, and wrote, in the column ruled for it, no reply, and closed the book, and looked entirely at peace.

"Front's coming in," he said, cocking the free ear at the roof. "Proper end of days out there tomorrow. We'll go down together in the morning."

Seppe slept on the orange couch, under the blanket kept folded on its arm, and the last thing he heard was the sea that wasn't there.

They came down off the ridge in the morning ahead of the rain, the radio man walking, Seppe ranging ahead and back with the lance the way Sirius ranged ahead and back on the trails, and below them, down the long green throat of the valley, Querencia came up out of the fog: the terraces, the fountain square, the long house with its chimney already working.

The mesh chimed in the radio man's pocket at the tree line. He read it, and huffed the small laugh of a man caught by an ambush he walks into weekly, and turned the slate so Seppe could see.

talvin. you're cooking saturday. no radishes this time. we have VOTED. ⁓ a

"There was nothing wrong with the radishes," Talvin said, with dignity, and put the slate away, and went down the last of the hill toward the long house, where the door was open, and the tables were loud, and forty people who all knew exactly where he was when he wasn't at dinner were keeping his seat.

Fourteen years ago, for one night, on another island, he had been the one person left at the exact center of everything, with no line remaining to call a soul.

Every soul he called now answered.

A few words for the curious

Radio words, mostly, and one long-kept secret let out.

  • CQ: the oldest call in radio ⁓ the general call, sent to no one and everyone: calling any station. Is anybody there. Operators have opened with it for more than a century. The radio man sends it at the end of every session, three times, into ten years of static, and logs the silence, and is at peace. Make of that ritual what you will; he has never explained it, and we won't either.
  • The bands as terrain: real radio physics, shepherd's translation ⁓ high frequencies travel line of sight like high passes; low bands go slow around the mountain; and the ionosphere is a fog that sometimes reflects a whisper across an ocean. Conditions change nightly. "Conditions," said in a certain tone, is a festival announcement.
  • Salto del pastor: the Canarian shepherd's leap ⁓ the long lance, the plant, the flight. Installment fourteen's Hermanos Chacho perform it. Seppe Rosette commutes with it.
  • The finca: a hundred-some years of stone above San José de los Llanos, re-roofed in salvage, restrung in wire. In any wind the antenna spans sing ⁓ a low chord like the sea in places that don't have one.
  • And yes: the radio man is Talvin Jelfs ⁓ installment thirteen's night watchman at the Bude landing station, the man who watched the lines go dark with no one left to call. He worked passage on a fishing boat and came ashore at Los Gigantes in the last winter the boats still came, because a friend had once written come see the volcano, and he knew which island she meant. The laminated sheet by the window is the one from the landing station. Every number on it is dead. He kept it anyway ⁓ see the ritual, above. He has been at the long table the whole time. He cooked the harvest dinner. He makes, by unanimous informal ruling, entirely acceptable stew, radishes notwithstanding.
