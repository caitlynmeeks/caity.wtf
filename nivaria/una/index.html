Episode 21

Una

Querencia de los Helechos, the onion terraces. November, 2041 ❧ planting week.

Welcome to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Caitlyn Meeks. Thanks for being here.

The black cat came for Saga at first light, which had never once happened before.

The arrangement, as Saga understood it, ran the other way. The black cat with the limp belonged to the Cebolla sisters' colony, insofar as any cat has ever belonged to anything, and for as long as Saga could remember it had simply known when she was nearby ❧ materializing on the wall by the fork in the path, mid-wash, pretending the timing was coincidence. It did not come to people. It arranged to be come upon.

But that morning, in a fog so thick the fountain sounded like it was in the next valley, the cat was on the sill of Saga's round window ❧ two floors up, don't ask ❧ looking in at her with the specific patience of a creature that has decided to be understood.

"What," said Saga.

The cat dropped from the sill, and waited, and looked back once, and Saga, thirteen and a half and raised properly, put her boots on.

The cat took her up the path with its limping busy walk, past the fork, along the wall of the colony where a dozen other cats sat weirdly still, all of them facing the same direction like compass needles, and down the stone steps into the sisters' onion terraces, where the year's planting stood in its first week ❧ soft turned rows, seed onions tucked in at intervals, everything gray and dripping and quiet.

And out of that quiet, from somewhere down among the rows, came the laughing.

Saga stopped walking. It is a sound you stop walking for. It came up through the fog in long unhinged ribbons ❧ a cackling, sobbing, gasping laugh, the laugh of something that has just heard the funniest thing in the world and is not entirely coping with it ❧ and it was a sound she knew, almost. She had heard it twice in her life, at dusk, off the west coast rocks, coming from far out on the darkening water where the rafts gather. She had never heard it from inside an onion field.

The cat sat down at the terrace lip, duty discharged, and began to wash.

Saga went down the rows on her toes, and found it in the third one: a big gray-brown seabird sitting in the soft dirt between two planted onions, wings folded wrong, looking up at her with a dark wet eye ❧ a pardela. A young one, down from this year's cliffs, big as a cat and built entirely for a life that contains no dirt of any kind: wings like a schooner, legs set so far back on the hull that on land it could only push itself along on its own keel. Somewhere in the night, on the first flight of its life, the fog or the wind or plain inexperience had put it down here, twelve kilometers and seven hundred meters of altitude from the nearest thing it understood.

It could not take off again. Not from flat ground; the design does not allow it. It could only sit in the world's largest onion field and laugh.

"Okay," said Saga, who knew what you did, because everyone on the island over a certain age had made sure everyone under it knew what you did. You took off your overshirt. You dropped it over the bird like a curtain falling on an act. You gathered it up like a loaf of bread, firm, both hands, wings pinned kindly ❧ and you kept your fingers away from the front end, because a pardela's bill is a fish-hook with confidence, and it will close on a knuckle like a stapler and consider the matter settled.

The bundle laughed against her ribs the whole way up the steps, and the black cat walked ahead of them, tail up, escorting.

❦

The sisters' yard sat above the colony wall, behind a gate Saga came through sideways, guarding the front end of her cargo, and the morning received her three completely different ways at once.

Remedios came out of the shed first: a tiny wire of a woman, brown as a nut, forearms like mooring rope, carrying a feed sack that outweighed her and not appearing to have noticed. She has one facial expression. She has had it, as far as anyone knows, since before the ferries stopped, and the island long ago gave up on the face and learned to read her eleven silences instead. She looked at the laughing bundle in Saga's arms and deployed silence number four, the one that means bring it to the table.

Angustias filled the kitchen doorway behind her, built on the scale of church architecture, hair the color of a rusted roof pinned up with whatever had been nearest at dawn ❧ that morning, visibly, a pencil and a fork. She took one look at the bundle and said, with relish, "It's dead."

The bundle laughed.

"Nearly dead," said Angustias, unbothered. And this, Saga would confirm over the nine days that followed, is the whole engine of Angustias: prophecy, doom, specifics, delivered with the deep pleasure of a woman finally proven right; refuted on the spot by the world; and entered anyway in her running ledger of near misses. The fog will take the row ❧ the row survives ❧ a near miss. The bird is dead ❧ the bird laughs ❧ nearly dead. She has been almost right about everything since at least 2019, and no failure has ever dented her, because catastrophe, for Angustias, is not a forecast so much as a devotion.

And Milagros was already at the table under the fig tree, though nobody had seen her cross the yard. Nobody ever has. She is tall and narrow as a cypress, and her gray braid hangs to the backs of her knees, and the colony's kittens regard the braid as public transport, so that she moves through her day with one or two riding it like a funicular, and does not appear to notice, and possibly does not.

"Una pardela," she said, quietly, before the overshirt had so much as twitched.

She said it the old way, too ❧ una, the article with a number folded inside it, grammar the rest of the island flattened down to e a generation ago. The sisters keep the old articles the way they keep seed onions. It is, Saga has decided, a kind of politeness to things: to say not just bird but one bird, this one, singular, and mean it.

About the aprons, which match: blue, white rickrack, three of them, and no one has ever established where a third matching apron came from, or in what order the aprons and the sisters found each other. They are identical, and they are the easiest way to tell the sisters apart. Remedios's is immaculate, creased like a uniform, and has been since the world ended. Angustias's is the event archive ❧ every stain a story, every story told at length, on request or without it ("that one is the goat wedding, I'll come back to it"). And Milagros's is inside out. It is always inside out. No one has ever seen her put it on. There are standing theories, and they are all wrong, probably, like the other thing everyone believes: all three sisters claim to be the youngest. All three are lying. Probably.

The table cleared, a folded blanket went down, and the overshirt lifted away like the curtain going up.

The pardela sat on the blanket in the gray morning light, enormous and wrong and perfect, and laughed at all of them.

"Dying," said Angustias, revising downward as the light hit it. "Look at the eye. That's a dying eye. I've seen that eye."

"You've seen your own eye," said Remedios. "Hold the light."

They went over the bird the way the sisters went over everything, which is to say three ways at once. Remedios spread each wing to its whole absurd length and walked her fingers down the long bones the way you'd check a roof beam, pressing, listening with her hands. Her instruments came out of a leather roll that also does the village haircuts on Sundays and the sardine work on weekdays. "Different scissors," she said, to a look Saga had not managed to hide, and went back to the wing. Nobody on the island believes the scissors are different. The haircuts, in fairness, are excellent. Angustias held the light and provided the prognosis, which was dire, and revised it every few seconds, downward. And Milagros did not appear to do anything at all ❧ she simply stood at the head of the table with one finger resting on the bird's breast, and the bird, which had laughed at the fog and laughed at the onions and laughed against Saga's ribs the whole way up the hill, went quiet under her finger like a kettle taken off the flame, and looked at her, and let all of it happen.

Saga filed that. She had been filing it for years without knowing what the file was called: the animals go still for Milagros. All of them. Every time.

"Nothing broken," said Remedios at last, folding the second wing closed like a deck chair. "Bruised in the shoulder. Fat, actually. A good year on the cliffs."

"Starving," Angustias corrected, for the record.

"Fat. It needs a week, fish, and a cliff with wind on it." Remedios looked at the bird with the flat tenderness she otherwise reserved for a clean splice in an irrigation line. "Grounded, is all. First flight. The fog put it in our onions like a letter in a box."

"In the before," Angustias said, suddenly not performing at all, "October meant boxes." And for a moment nobody said anything, because it did ❧ boxes in every stairwell on the coast, boxes in bars, the year's young pardelas raining down wherever the streetlights were, hundreds of them, whole campaigns of schoolchildren carrying them to the sea in cardboard. The lights went out with everything else. The island went dark, and the fledglings stopped falling, and this fat laughing accident in the onions was the first one anyone had brought the sisters in six years.

The bird chose that silence to laugh again, hugely, at length, from the very bottom of its being.

"Yes," agreed Angustias, wiping her eyes. "Well. You would know."

❦

A seabird eats fish, and Querencia sits seven hundred meters above the nearest fish, and this is the kind of problem the island has stopped noticing it solves.

Remedios wrote four words on the mesh board that evening. By the second morning there was a salt tin riding up from the coast on the Guagua with two kilos of sardines in it and a note from nobody, which is how the island signs most of its gifts. Fran handed the tin down from the driver's window without being asked, the way you'd hand over mail.

Feeding a pardela is an act of faith. You hold the sardine at the correct angle and you present it to the front end, the end with the fish-hook, and there is a moment where the bird regards you and the sardine and its own options, and then the stapler closes and the fish is a memory and you count your fingers out of old habit. On the third day the stapler closed on Angustias instead of the sardine, and she stood in the yard holding her knuckle to the sky and delivered a eulogy for the finger, its long life, its many services to the family, while Remedios said "other angle" without looking up and Milagros fed the bird the rest of the tin, one fish at a time, in perfect peace.

The bird lived in the convalescent shed with the airy slats, and at three in the morning it laughed.

It laughed the way lighthouses sweep ❧ long, unhurried, on a schedule known only to itself ❧ and the sound rolled out of the shed and across the colony wall, where forty cats sat up in the dark and took it personally, and on down through the fog to the village, where people woke, and listened, and grinned in their beds, and went back to sleep. A seabird's night-laugh, seven hundred meters up a mountain, in the middle of a laurisilva forest. Nobody complained. For one week in November, Querencia fell asleep inside a sound that had not been heard that far from the water in anyone's memory, and the village, without discussing it, decided it was lucky.

The planting went on around all of it, because planting week does not pause for comedy.

Saga worked the rows with the sisters in the afternoons ❧ seed onions from the flat trays, a hand's width apart, firmed in with two knuckles, don't bury the neck ❧ and the yard talked its three ways over the work: Remedios in imperatives and measurements, Milagros barely at all, and Angustias in forecasts. "That fog is coming down," she announced on the second afternoon, straightening with a hand in her back. "By four. We lose the row. Mark it." The fog lifted while she was still pointing at it. She marked it anyway, as a near miss.

And it was that same afternoon, when the argument came through ❧ the argument, the one the whole village knows about, running since 2019, subject unknown; it comes in bands, like the fog, and burns off for work ❧ that Saga finally saw the famous thing happen up close.

The water barrels had to come up from the lower cistern, one on each end of a pole, and Remedios and Angustias took the pole together, still arguing, and lifted ❧

❧ and the argument became a song.

Mid-sentence. Mid-word, very nearly. No look passed between them, no breath was taken; one instant it was the argument, and the next it was something Canarian and older than the argument, Remedios walking the low line of it and Angustias sailing above her, two voices under one pole finding the exact rhythm the load wanted, and the barrels came up the steps on that rhythm like they weighed nothing worth mentioning.

They set the pole down on the top terrace, and the song un-converted, mid-verse, into the argument ❧ at, as far as Saga could tell, the exact word it had left.

She watched it happen four more times that week. It never varied. Load on: song. Load off: argument, resumed to the syllable, as if the song were a room the argument stepped into whenever there was lifting to do, and stepped out of after, wiping its feet.

Anna came up on the Thursday with a barrow of spent candy-mash for the compost, and stayed an hour, and was, as ever, no use to the argument whatsoever. Anna agrees with people the way rain gets things wet, indiscriminately and sincerely, and at one point she agreed with both sisters inside a single sentence, and the argument stalled, lost its footing entirely, and had to be restarted later from memory. She went home glowing, with onions, and the sisters watched her down the steps with the tenderness people keep for the one neighbor nobody has ever managed to fight with.

It was while planting out the last tray, knuckle-deep in the cold ground next to Remedios, that Saga asked her first question.

"What's the argument about?"

"A ladder," said Remedios, not stopping. "She returned it wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong."

And that appeared to be the entire ladder.

❦

She asked Angustias two days later, drying sardine tins at the outdoor sink, and got a different argument entirely.

"The ladder?" Angustias flicked water off a tin with real scorn. "The ladder was nothing. The ladder was resolved." She leaned in, checked the yard for her sister with the instincts of a smuggler, and lowered her voice to a volume slightly above her normal speaking voice. "It's the song."

"The song?"

"She sings the harmony flat. On purpose. Since 2019." Angustias let that land with the gravity it deserved. "Not much flat. Exactly flat enough. You understand? Flat enough that I can hear it, not flat enough that I could ever prove it. For six years." She straightened up, wrung out the cloth like a neck. "You could hear it yourself, if you had serious ears. A quarter of a quarter of a tone. Like a table with one short leg that only wobbles when company comes."

Saga walked home down the steps that evening with her brain doing a slow, delighted loop, because if Angustias was right, then the thing the argument turned into under load ❧ the song, the beautiful weatherlike song that carried the barrels up the terraces ❧ was the thing the argument was about. They fought their way into it and sang their way back out of it and the song they took shelter in was the battlefield. She listened hard at the next conversion, both ears, serious as she knew how to be. It sounded the way weather sounds: correct.

Milagros she almost didn't ask.

It took her the rest of the week to work up to it, which she couldn't explain, except that questions went quiet around Milagros the same way animals did. She found her at last in the convalescent shed at dusk, sitting on an upturned crate while the pardela stood on the cool floor working its wings ❧ great slow sweeps, again, again, the wind of it stirring the straw ❧ and Saga sat down on the other crate, and watched with her, and finally asked the wall more than the woman:

"What's the argument about?"

Milagros considered it. She considered it the way she considered an injured wing, honestly, from several sides, taking her time. The pardela folded itself and looked from one of them to the other.

"What argument?" she said.

And the terrible thing, Saga thought, the truly excellent and terrible thing, was that she meant it. It was not a deflection. It was a real question, asked with real curiosity, by a woman who had lived in the same small house as the argument for six years, and it dismantled Saga's entire investigation the way you'd take the last stone out of an arch.

That night she laid the three answers side by side in her notebook: a ladder, returned wrong. The harmony, flat on purpose. What argument? Three answers, each certain, each impossible beside the others, and above all three of them the argument itself sailing on across the terraces, high and low, entirely well. She drew a box around all of it and did not draw any lines between. Some cases you close. Some you keep open on purpose, coming back now and then to touch the evidence, the way the sisters kept their seed onions ❧ not unfinished. Kept.

It was the pardela that called the end of the week.

The wing-work at dusk got longer and stronger, the straw flying now, the whole shed drumming with it. The bird ate like a harbor. And on the ninth night the laughing changed ❧ Saga heard it from her loft and sat straight up in the dark, because there was a direction in it now, a lean, the way a moored boat leans when the tide turns underneath it. It laughed toward the sea.

In the morning Milagros stood in the shed doorway for a while, one finger on the bird's breast, listening to whatever she listened to.

"Tonight," she said.

❦

They took the community's old Series II down the mountain after dark, Remedios driving, Angustias navigating in the sense that she foretold doom at each bend and was each time proven wrong, logging every survival as a near miss, Saga wedged in the back between the apron shoulders with the box on her knees. The box laughed at the switchbacks. The fog thinned, and gave out, and the sky over the coast opened up black and enormous and full of stars, because there is nothing anywhere on the island anymore to dim them.

Garachico at night is a town with its lights down low and its history laid out flat to the sea ❧ the old lava tongue of 1706 gone green and settled, the rock pools breathing in the dark. A town that has already survived its own apocalypse once, rebuilt on top of the thing that tried to end it, and is therefore surprised by nothing. Three women, a girl, and a box of laughter coming down to the water at ten at night did not raise an eyebrow that Saga could detect. An old man fishing the rocks moved his bucket to give them the point, and that was Garachico's entire commentary.

The wind came off the sea steady and cool, smelling of the whole rest of the world.

Remedios opened the box and went over the wings one last time, beam by beam, and folded them back and nodded. Angustias looked at the bird for a long moment and had, for once, no prognosis at all ❧ or she had one, and it was too big to perform, which for Angustias is the same as silence. And Milagros lifted the pardela out and carried it to the edge of the black rock, and stood with it raised against the wind, her finger on its breast one last time, the sea moving below them like something turning over in its sleep.

The bird went quiet. The wind filled its feathers.

She opened her hands.

There was one bad half-second ❧ a drop, a lurch, the old design remembering itself ❧ and then the wings caught, and the pardela went out low across the water and was part of the dark before anyone's breath came back. Gone. Completely, instantly, the way sea things go.

And then, from far out, the laugh came back to them ❧ once, long, unhinged, trailing away over the water like a ribbon let go into the wind. And farther out than that, something answered it. Or the water did. It was dark; you may choose.

"Una," said Remedios, to the sea, counting it out properly.

They stood a while, because you do.

On the way back up the mountain the argument resumed somewhere around the second switchback, low and high, comfortable as the engine. And then, halfway up through the fog belt, with nothing in the cab to lift and no barrels and no poles and no crates for miles, it converted ❧ mid-sentence, into the song, one voice walking and one voice sailing, all the way up through the dripping forest to home.

Saga rode inside it and did not ask.

The black cat was on the wall by the gate when they came in, mid-wash, timing it perfectly, pretending coincidence. It got the last of the sardines, and so did the colony, and the empty convalescent shed stood open to air out, and for a few nights afterward Querencia slept a little shallowly, missing a sound it had only owned for nine days and had already filed under ours.

In her loft, by lantern, Saga added one line under the three answers in her notebook, and boxed it with the rest, and went to sleep.

All of them are true. That's what the aprons match for.

She was thirteen. She reserved the right to revise it.

A few words for the curious

Spanish and Guanche-adjacent this time, and one piece of grammar.

  • Pardela: the Cory's shearwater ❧ the big gray seabird of the Canary night, wings like a schooner, legs built for water and catastrophic on land. Its call is famous: a wild, sobbing, entirely unreasonable laughing, usually heard after dark off the coasts. In the before, the October streetlights grounded the young ones by the hundred, and the islands ran rescue campaigns ❧ schoolchildren, cardboard boxes, releases at the shore. The lights are gone now. The fledglings keep the dark and make the sea. The boxes are not missed, exactly. But the people who carried them remember carrying them.
  • Una: the feminine singular article, with the number one folded inside it. La Charla flattened most articles down to a single all-purpose e a generation ago; the Cebolla sisters keep the old ones in working order, the way they keep seed onions and matching aprons. To say una pardela instead of e pardela is to count the thing you are looking at: one bird. This one. Singular.
  • Remedios, Angustias, Milagros: remedies, anguishes, miracles ❧ the old Marian names, and among the three of them a complete theory of care. What the sisters are to one another has never been settled, least of all by the sisters, and the island long ago concluded that the aprons match and that this is answer enough.
  • The 1706 lava: Garachico was the island's richest port until the volcano sent a tongue of lava through it and into the sea. The town rebuilt on top of the flow. The rock pools where the pardela went back to the water are the cooled edge of the thing that once tried to erase the town ❧ which is the kind of joke this island tells slowly, over three hundred years.
