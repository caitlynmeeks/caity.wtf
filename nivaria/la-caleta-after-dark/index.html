Episode 5

La Caleta, After Dark

An early cut — this one is still being tuned.

La Caleta kept its boats right there at the edge of things — small wooden hulls painted blue and green and a red so bright it looked borrowed from somewhere wetter, pulled up on the shingle where the town met the water. The square behind them had been a parking lot once, back when people still drove everywhere; now it was dust and warmth and the smell of honey warming under cloth awnings, and a puppet stage folded down to its planks while kids ran between the stalls with the particular energy of children who'd just seen something they couldn't stop talking about.

Anna had come down off her mountain for the big market — a few times each season, the way the moon pulls a tide — and she'd set up near that folded stage under a cloth awning with hundreds of small brightly wrapped confections piled in glass jars and baskets. The kids were already three deep at her table, still breathless from the show, trading stories for candy: a twist of blackberry taffy for a good one, an anise drop for a worse one. She'd watched the whole puppet play from behind them, over their heads, arms folded and chin down — and Saga, still gathering felt tadpoles from her fingers backstage, had heard Anna's laughter landing a half-second before everyone else's, every single time. Not late, the way strangers laugh, catching up to a joke. Early. The way you only laugh at a story you knew before anybody else did.

Saga slipped the last tadpole into its pouch and stepped out from behind the folded stage. Her slate buzzed against her hip. She dug it out. On the little grey screen, arriving the slow way — cantenna to cantenna, pylon to Pringles-can pylon, all the way down from the cloud forest to the dry coast — was a message in her mother's particular shorthand.

¿cómo va todo? show the little ones the rabbit if the sky goes clear tonight. — m

Saga thumbed back: first show done. nobody died. Detective Ardilla is famous. sky's too bright here for rabbits. tell maddy I said hi.

She watched it leave — three small dots, then gone, off up the mountain to a kitchen and a server and a woman with mud on her boots — and felt the odd, good largeness of being a long way from home and still, somehow, inside it. The mesh didn't carry much. It carried enough.

And Gertie was still on the floor behind the folded stage with her hand up inside the Maisie puppet. She had the popped eye between two fingers and a little tube of glue on the concrete, pressing the bead back into its felt socket — holding it, thumb very still while the glue took, the way you hold a splinter out of a smaller person's hand. She didn't put the puppet down to do it. She mended the eye with the squirrel still on her arm.

Saga knew the squirrel as long as she knew Gertie. Maisie was painted into the corner of half the dead walls on the island — on the seaward side of this very square, if you walked around to look: a squirrel in a too-big hat with a magnifying glass, signed Plasma-G in small clean letters, the same hand, the same patient love, town after town after town.

Anna came and found her before the gold went out of the afternoon. She'd left the candy table to the kids and came down the row still wiping sugar off her hands, and she didn't say good job, the thing grown-ups say. She crouched down to Saga's level and looked at her dead serious.

"Greta. We had one job."

She let it sit a second.

"That wasn't in the show. You put that in."

Saga felt her whole face go hot. "It just kind of came out."

"I know it did. That's writing for you." Anna held her eyes a beat longer, and something crossed her face that Saga didn't have a name for — proud, and under the proud something further off, like a light left on in a room nobody's used in years. "You did great," she said, lighter now, getting back up with a hand on her knee. "And we're definitely adding your line to the script. With your permission of course."

Saga laughed and nodded.

Then Anna went over and lowered herself onto the warm concrete beside Gertie, and the two of them looked at the squirrel together — Anna at the mended eye, Gertie at nothing in particular.

"She held up," Anna said.

"She always does," said Gertie. "We built her to."

We. Saga caught the word and didn't ask about it. There was a third person folded into that we, she was pretty sure — somebody who wasn't down here on the dry coast and wasn't up in the green either, somebody whose name Saga only ever met in two places: clean small letters on the lid of an old game, and the slow, careful sentences of a machine that lived in a stable behind a kitchen. The quiet between the two women had that person's shape in it, and neither of them filled it in. You don't, Saga was learning, with the ones you miss the most. You leave the chair empty where it is.

She got up and went to find out whether the honey people had brought the kind with the comb still in it, because she was thirteen and a half and starving and the show was over. And behind her Gertie held the little glued eye a moment longer than a puppet's eye needs holding, and Anna sat with her and didn't hurry her, and the bassline rolled out across the warm concrete, and the dry afternoon went, despite everything the south of the island could do about it, gold.

The market folded itself up like a well-used map — awnings rolling in, tables sliding shut, the IKEA chair man shouldering his prize and vanishing between two buildings. The honey people gave Saga a thin wedge of comb on a scrap of wax paper, and she walked with it down the row, chewing, letting the sweetness run over her tongue while the square emptied around her.

La Caleta kept its fishing boats right there at the edge of things — small wooden hulls painted blue and green and a red so bright it looked borrowed from somewhere wetter, pulled up on the shingle where the town met the water. The day's catch already sold or traded, but the paint stayed. Saga walked past them with the comb still in her teeth, the wood smelling of tar and salt and something older, like the boats were telling the same story the market did: we're still here, we're still doing this.

A few dead cars sat out on the dirt beyond the square's edge — stripped for wiring, doors gone, one of them wearing a coat of rust so thick it looked deliberate. They'd been part of the parking lot once, before the parking lot became a plaza. Now they were just scenery, something for goats to climb and kids to pretend was a spaceship.

The bassline found her again — Mateo's dubstep, rolling out from the rover's tailgate in a low pulse that didn't ask permission, just pulled. It wasn't fast music. It was patient music, the kind that walks into your chest and sits down. Saga felt it in her sternum before she heard it properly, a vibration more than a melody, calling the grown-ups the way the puppets had called the kids.

She followed it.

The path down to the beach wasn't long — steep and windy in the real world, but here it felt like stepping from one room into another. The dry south coast opened up as you walked: cardón and tabaiba scrub on either side, the sky going orange and then pink and then a blue so deep it looked like somebody'd been saving it all day for after dark. Somebody handed Saga a cup — warm ceramic, something fruity and sharp that made her tongue curl at the sides — and she took it without asking what it was because that was how La Tropa worked: you were given things, and you accepted them, and later you figured out if you liked them.

By the time her feet hit sand, the sky had gone clear. No clouds. No haze from the north's fog. Just stars, already pricking through, and the dark shape of the cliffs rising on either side like shoulders holding the cove open for whatever came next.

The cove breathed. Two hundred people, maybe more, spread across the sand in clusters — some dancing, some sitting on salvaged blankets, some standing around a fire that wasn't Yara's yet but would be. The air smelled of salt and kerosene and something sweet roasting over coals farther down the beach. Saga stood at the edge of it all, the warm cup still in her hands, feeling the music move through the crowd like blood through a body.

By the brazier, Tibor and Yara were building the night between them. Tibor — who ran La Tropa and looked like somebody'd crossed a Hungarian folk-bear with a software engineer, all belly and beard and round little glasses — walked a string of his homemade LED juggling clubs through a slow figure, half rehearsal, half showing off. The clubs woke warm in his hands and drifted through colors that didn't have names — a slow aurora he could throw, green and amber spinning loose through the dark. He narrated the whole time a story that had nothing to do with juggling, something about a bridge and a troll and a toll you paid in riddles, his Hungarian accent thickening when he got to the good parts.

Beside him Yara knelt over the poi — unusually tall, pale blonde dreadlocks catching the firelight, the one who'd taught Saga both fire and tarot — dunking each wick in lamp-oil. She breathed in, held, breathed out, the same rounds her mother had used to cross a frozen lake on camera. Then she tested a wick against her lighter. A hard bright flower of real flame stood up in the dusk and went out under her glove. She looked up at the sky, checked it for wind, nodded to herself.

"Not yet," she said to Tibor. "Let them get cold first."

"They are already cold," Tibor said, not stopping his clubs. "I am watching them shiver."

"They're dancing. That's not shivering."

"It is very close shivering."

Saga laughed into her cup. The drink was warm now from her hands, and it tasted less like fruit and more like belonging — the particular flavor of a place where nobody asked why you were laughing, they just laughed too.

Mateo crouched at the rover's tailgate over the battery and the panels, and the dub bassline rolled out across the sand in a wave that made people move without deciding to. Not dancing-dancing, not yet. Just swaying, leaning, letting the music hold them up for a second. Mateo didn't look up from his knobs and wires. He wore the same expression he'd worn as Reginald Glidewell three hours ago — total concentration — but now it was kind. The same focus, pointed at making people feel good instead of making them laugh at a villain.

Saga found Luca near the waterline, sitting with his boots off and his feet in the sand, drawing circles with a stick. Stef's youngest, twelve years old and born right into the quiet, still grinning from the foghorn bottle. He'd traded it for a pair of drumsticks now, tapping a soft rhythm against a piece of driftwood that matched Mateo's bassline without trying.

"Hey," Saga said, dropping down beside him.

"Hey."

"You were good today. The foghorn."

He shrugged, but his ears went pink. "Gertie said I made her laugh too early. Messed up her cue."

"You did. It was great."

They sat there a minute, the water coming in cold over their ankles, the sand pulling out from under their heels with each retreat. The sky had gone fully dark now — no moon yet, or maybe it was hiding behind the cliffs — and the stars were everywhere, thick as spilled salt.

Saga remembered her mother's message.

She stood up, brushing sand off her knees, and went to find the little ones. They were clustered at the edge of the firelight, the same kids who'd watched Maisie Ardilla lose her eye, now running on sugar and adrenaline and the particular magic of being allowed to stay up past dark. Saga crouched down among them, pointing up at the sky.

"See those three stars there? In a row?"

"That's Orion's belt," said a small girl with braids. "Everybody knows that."

"Okay, smarty. Now look below it. See that fuzzy patch?"

They squinted. She pointed her finger, tracing the shape.

"Four stars making a diamond. Two ears up top. That's the rabbit. Lepus. He's running from Orion's dogs — see, those bright ones to the left? They're chasing him forever but they never catch him."

"Why not?"

"Because he's fast," Saga said. "And because the sky likes a good chase."

The small girl looked at her seriously. "That's not scientific."

"No," Saga agreed. "But it's true."

Behind them, Yara's first fire arc went up — a bloom of orange and gold against the black sky, trailing sparks that died before they hit the sand. The crowd made a sound that wasn't quite words. Tibor's clubs answered in their wordless colors, green and amber spinning through the dark, his invented light dancing alongside her true fire.

Saga stood up, the little ones forgotten already, chasing each other down to the water. She found Anna again near the cave mouth — seven layers of fluorescent paint glowing in constellations Tibor had mapped from memory, Linolada's pallet-wood bar warm under her palms, the brazier burning brezo so the air smelled of heather and salt and something older. No profit and no prices, just give-and-take — and Anna handed her a strip of pork grilled over charcoal without asking if she was hungry.

She was. She always was, at these things. Not for food, or not only. For the warmth of bodies that had chosen each other, the smell of brezo smoke from Tibor's brazier mixing with salt and kerosene, the sand pulling slowly out from under her heels with each retreat of the water.

The rabbit ran overhead. The fire bloomed and died and bloomed again. And Saga, thirteen and a half, full of honeycomb and something sharper than she was used to, danced with her shoes off in the sand until her feet went numb and the bass in Mateo's speakers lived in her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Three kilometers up the slope, in a white concrete bunker on the razed bones of a farmhouse he'd once promised to restore, Klaus sat at his terrace table with the four Wedgwood cups and the last Lavazza tin and watched his golf course fill with strangers through binoculars that had cost more than the jeep he no longer had fuel for.

He could hear them even through the sealed patio doors: Mateo's dub, rolling up the hill like tide. Katzenmusik. His grandfather's word for any noise made by people under forty with amplifiers and no sheet music.

The fairways were ruined. He knew it without going down there — the tread of two hundred feet on grass that needed six more months to recover from the last drought, the scattered embers from their fires, and the goats. Always the goats. A small herd of them had settled into the ninth sand trap like it was a spa built specifically for them, and one was scratching its horns against the remains of a tee-box sign he'd installed himself.

His course. His investment. His Bienestar development, stalled now a decade and a half while the island pretended it didn't need capital anymore.

"He's down there," Klaus said.

Sonja stood at the concrete railing, rolling a cigarette from her pot-grown tobacco. She didn't turn around.

"With them," Klaus said. "Dancing with the verdammt cave hippies. On my greens. Like it's theirs."

Sonja licked the paper shut. She struck the match, let it flare, let it die. "He's gone," she said. "And it is theirs now." She drew smoke, looked at the sand trap where a second goat had settled in beside the first. "Accept it. Have some chicory. Watch the goats."

Klaus said nothing. His jaw worked. He reached for his notebook — leather-bound, pages filled with projections in his cramped hand: Q3 2034, then Q4 2036, now Q4 2046. Wenn alles zurückkommt. When everything comes back.

"The investors will return," he said. "The flights. The people who understand what a development opportunity looks like." He raised the binoculars again. "And he'll need somewhere else to play with his fire."

Sonja looked at the back of his head. "Your irrigation line still runs," she said. "Feeds the cave people, actually." She drew smoke. "You're the only developer I know who waters his own competition."

Klaus said nothing. His jaw tightened.

Sonja flicked ash toward the sand-filled pool and didn't say what else she was thinking. She'd learned that too.

A few words for the curious

Mostly English, with the south coast's dust on it — and a little German grumbling from higher ground.

  • La Caleta [la kah-LEH-ta] — a real former fishing village on Tenerife's dry southwest coast. In 2041 its town square — once a parking lot, back when people still had cars — hosts the big market and the puppet stage. The colorful wooden fishing boats still work; the dead cars stripped for wiring are just scenery now.
  • Playa de Diego Hernández [DEE-eh-go er-NAN-dez] / La Enramada — a real cove below Golf Costa Adeje, steep and windy to reach. In our 2041 it's where the party goes after the market winds down: Tibor's hand-dug cave bar, Yara's fire, Mateo's dub set on the sand.
  • Katzenmusik — German, literally "cat music." What Klaus calls any amplified noise he didn't approve in writing three weeks ahead. An authentic grumpy-German dismissal; his grandfather's word before him.
  • The mesh — the island's homemade nervous system: line-of-sight radio links hopping cantenna to cantenna across the peaks, a good number built from Pringles cans. It carries text, mostly. Text is enough.
  • Market days — La Tropa's circuit is timed to the markets. Most weeks the plaza market is modest; once a month each town throws the big one, when stalls double and people come in from the edges to sell a month's work in an afternoon. Where there's a show there's a market, and where there's the big market, there's a show.
  • The rabbit — Lepus, the constellation below Orion's belt. In Saga's family it's a story her mother tells: the hare running forever from the hunter's dogs, never caught because the sky likes a good chase. Not scientific. But true.

And yes — if you noticed that the puppet show's villain and the man on the balcony share a certain family resemblance, you're not wrong. Some stories are worth borrowing. We returned this one with a tree in it; tonight we borrowed another, and left a question mark where the period should be.
