Episode 12

Warmth Is the Lie

North. Very far north. There is snow, and a woman standing barefoot in it, and she would like a word with you. The year is 2024.

Welcome to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Caitlyn Meeks. Thanks for being here.

The snow went on forever, and the woman stood in the middle of it with no shoes.

She stood on one leg. The other foot rested flat against the standing knee, her hands together at her chest the way people pray in places with incense, and her eyes were closed. Tree pose, barefoot, in snow. She held it without effort and without hurry, like stillness was a thing she owned and was letting you look at.

She was sixty, or near it, and she'd arranged her whole life so you would never guess. Pale hair to her waist. A man's reindeer-hide hat that no one entitled to give it had given her. Bare feet on the white, pink at the heel, perfectly still. Behind her a lake lay under a skin of ice, and the light coming off it was the kind of blue that makes people lower their voices.

The camera came in close. Her eyelashes were frosted white, every one of them distinct. And while you were admiring them, her eyes came open ❧ all at once, both together, nothing gradual about it.

"Namaste," she said.

When she spoke again, she spoke slowly, the way the rich are slow, because nothing in her life had ever asked her to hurry.

"There is a warmth," she said, "that is killing you."

She let that sit on the snow a moment.

"You've felt it your whole life and you've called it comfort. The blanket. The bath. The hot meal at the end of a cold day. They told you that was love." A small smile, sorry for you. "It was an anesthetic. Heat is comfort. Comfort is sleep. And a sleeping animal never has to change."

She didn't shiver. That was the entire argument, and she let you watch her not make it.

"Cooking was the first drug we ever sold ourselves," she said. "Everything you call civilization happened while we were warm. In twenty-one days, outside Tromsø, I can teach you to set it down. To eat nothing warmer than the snow. To greet the four winds. To drink the water the aurora drinks." The smile again. "The cold doesn't care what you used to be. The cold only asks what you're willing to become. That is the kindest sentence anyone will ever say to you, and the world is too warm to hear it."

Then she breathed.

In through the nose, slow, until her shoulders rose and there was no more room. She held it at the top, where it had nowhere left to go. She let it down again, all the way to the bottom, past where most people stop.

"Breathe with me," she said. "It costs nothing. It's the only thing I'll ever give you for free."

And somebody a very long way from the snow ❧ in a room where the air was thirty degrees and going nowhere ❧ breathed in with her, and could not, for the life of her, stop.

The snow was four hundred pixels wide, and it stuttered. The woman froze and unfroze a tenth of a second at a time, the way tired video does on a tired connection. The blue light was a laptop screen. The ocean of the woman's breathing was a single speaker the size of a thumbnail, going tinny at the top of every inhale. A grey bar crawled along the bottom of her, and when it stalled she froze mid-breath into a small spinning ring ❧ serene, buffering, forever about to exhale.

The snow she stood in was rented. So was the lavvu behind the camera, which she called a yurt, from the Sámi herders who leased her their winter pasture and watched her greet the four winds on it with their faces carefully neutral. Asked, once, what they made of Wintersong Cryosattva, they had conferred and settled on the whole of it: she pays in advance.

It was not snowing. It had not snowed in this place in anyone's memory. It was the second week of Yara's life on the island, and the room around the laptop was a box of poured concrete in the hills above Adeje, and it was so hot that the floor tiles were the only cool thing in it.

So Yara sat on the floor. Sixteen years old, back against the foot of the bed, the laptop on her knees, watching her mother ❧ four thousand kilometers north and four months gone ❧ explain to strangers, for four thousand euros each, that warmth was a lie.

The laptop was a Chromebook, four years old, bought in the Netherlands in another life, slow the way old machines get slow ❧ patiently. The lid was a sedimentary record of her: anime characters, a cartoon shark, pride flags tucked into the corners, stickers laid over stickers where fifteen had been pasted down over thirteen. Klaus had tried to solve it in her first week: a new one, silver and expensive, presented still sealed in its wrap. The silver one sat unopened at the bottom of her closet and would go on sitting there. The Chromebook had made the move with her. The stickers were the part that worked.

Solveig Vinter had been Yara's mother for sixteen years and a prophet of the cold for three, and somewhere in there the two jobs had quietly traded places. Wintersong was nobody's invitation. It was hers: her invention, her business, her pyramid with her own name at the top. That winter she had gone north to run it in person, and the Arctic months had no room in them for a sixteen-year-old. So while her mother went up into the dark, Yara was sent down into the sun: a box of poured concrete in Los Menores, and a man downstairs who would not say his daughter's name. Her mother loved her. Yara never doubted it. She'd just learned what the love weighed at four thousand kilometers, the way you learn the size of a room you're locked in.

She noticed her own chest then ❧ going in through the nose, slow, all the way down, with the woman on the screen. She'd caught herself doing it three times that week. She made it stop. It was like trying to stop a yawn. The body had taken the one true thing her mother ever taught it and filed it somewhere her mother couldn't reach.

Through the sliding glass, out on the terrace, her father sat where he sat through the hot part of every afternoon: a folding chair, a tripod, a telescope, a pair of binoculars, and a bottle of the fizzy mineral water he had shipped in by the case from a celebrated spring in Baden-Württemberg. He watched the people who lived in vans and tents on the cliffs down at El Puertito, two kilometers off and three hundred meters down, and he narrated them.

"Look at this," he said, to no one, to the dog, to the island. "On my view." He said view the way other men said land. He'd paid for the view. He'd knocked down a hundred-and-forty-year-old farmhouse to get it ❧ the dressed-stone walls, the bread oven, all of it into a skip ❧ and poured this house in its place, and the only thing the view had in it now that he hadn't personally approved was forty people living for free on a beach he already owned the rights to flatten. "Animals," he said, comfortably. "When the next phase goes through, that's cleared. All of it. You'll see."

The next phase had been about to go through for four years. The pool had been going in next spring for four springs; the rectangle dug for it sat at the lip of the terrace, dry, half full of builder's sand and one optimistic lizard ❧ a hole shaped exactly like the thing it wasn't. The man had a telescope that could find the rings of Saturn and he pointed it, every single day, at the ground.

He turned his head toward the open door without taking his eye from it.

"Jürgen," he called. "Come and see what the hippies are doing to my beach."

Yara didn't move. She'd learned not to feel the name land. Mostly.

On the lounger in the shade, Sonja turned a page of nothing and didn't look up from her nails.

"Her name is Yara," she said.

Flat. Bored. Not a defense ❧ Sonja didn't do defenses. Just a tired woman correcting a tedious error, the way you'd correct a man who kept calling the dog the wrong breed. Sonja had wanted the farmhouse. She'd seen the listing photos before Klaus bought it ❧ the bougainvillea, the fig shade, the old bread oven ❧ and she'd married a developer picturing herself in something like that, and she lived in this instead, and she'd stopped pretending the concrete was anything but concrete. She and Yara were the two people that man's imaginary future had stranded in the same hot box. They both knew it. Neither of them had ever once said so.

And that ❧ her name is Yara, said to a magazine, to nobody, costing nothing ❧ landed in Yara's chest harder than any speech, precisely because it cost nothing. Because Sonja said it like it was simply true.

Then Versace decided the matter of where to sit.

The Pomeranian was five months old and already crossed the cool tile with the gravity of a small emperor. He ignored the man who fed him, ignored the woman who bought him, and climbed into Yara's lap, where he turned around twice, sighed an enormous sigh for so small an animal, and went warm and heavy against the one person in the house who had nowhere else to be. Yara put her hand on the little furnace of the puppy's back. Outside, down at the bottom of the plot, the one fig tree too much trouble to dig out moved its leaves in the only breeze on the property, green and warm and patient, throwing the same shade it had thrown for a hundred and forty years over a family that wasn't here anymore.

On the laptop, her mother breathed out at last, and started the round again.

2041. Seventeen years later, the heat went on forever, and the woman standing in the middle of it had no shoes.

The plaza at Callao Salvaje held the day's warmth the way a pan holds it after the stove is off. The sun had gone down behind La Gomera an hour ago and the sky had finished its long orange business and settled into a deep clean blue, and the big market was folding itself away around the lime tree ❧ awnings coming down, crates going up onto shoulders. Doña Pino, warm and round and glad of both, who had taught Spanish to two generations of the coast before the world went quiet, supervised the whole operation from her chair without having been asked to. In the cleared middle of the square, on the flat stones by the old lidded well, the fire act was being built.

Yara stood at the center of it, barefoot, on stones that still remembered noon. She was thirty-three years old and taller than everyone and at home in every centimeter of it. She liked this exact minute of the evening ❧ the held minute, after the juggling and the puppets and before the fire, when a crowd stops being an audience and becomes a circle.

Torbe had already taken his applause. He took applause after every act, his or not, and he took it the way he took everything, as a debt being correctly repaid. He was seventeen years old now, a senior Pomeranian with a puppy's vitality, which the island's children considered proof of a good life ❧ three kilos of electric blue in a ruff and epaulettes, bowing in four directions from atop a wooden crate, beneath a hand-painted sign so freighted with flourish that the letters had to fight their way out: TORBELLINO EL MAGNÍFICO. He had since retired to the top of Yara's backpack with his chin on the zipper, off duty, watching the fire get built with the professional interest of a colleague.

Saga stood at the edge of the firelight with an unlit poi in each hand.

She was thirteen and a half, and tonight the end of the closer was hers ❧ the handoff, the last figure, the real flame. She had known the pattern for a year. She had the wicks trimmed and the cords checked, and she was checking the knots a fourth time, which was not about the knots.

"Hey," Yara said.

"I know the pattern."

"I know you know the pattern."

"I'm not worried about the pattern," Saga said, in the voice of someone who was not going to say what she was worried about, because saying it might feed it.

"Come here," Yara said.

Saga came. Yara turned her so they both faced the fire pit and put one big hand flat on Saga's back, low, under the ribs.

"In through the nose," she said. "Four counts. Keep going. All the way, until there's no more room. Now hold it at the top. Seven. Don't grip it. Just hold it, like you'd hold a bird." A breath's worth of quiet. "Now let it down. Eight. Slow. All the way to the bottom. Past where you'd usually stop. There's more down there. Find the bottom of it."

Saga found the bottom of it.

"Again," Yara said. "Three more rounds. It costs nothing."

It was her mother's breath, the same rounds, the same hold, the same steadiness, and this was where it went now ❧ handed over free, in the dark, to a girl with fire in her near future.

They did the rounds. Somewhere in the third one Saga's shoulders came down from around her ears and arrived at the place shoulders belong, and she looked up at Yara with the look that meant okay and also don't make it a thing, and Yara did not make it a thing.

The fire act went the way the fire act goes when it goes right, which cannot really be written down. There was bass in it, and a wall of sparks off the staff, and the crowd's one long inhale when Yara pulled the first circle of flame out of the dark like something she had found there. And at the end, on the breath, she offered the lit poi across the space between them, and Saga took it. The handoff went the way it had gone since Saga was eight and it had been a candle: clean, unhurried, exactly on the exhale. The last figure of the night belonged to a thirteen-year-old with her shoulders down, and the crowd made the sound a town makes when it watches one of its own kids do a thing correctly over fire.

Afterward there were embers, and the market's last honey smell, and the crowd loosening into a party the way crowds did. Around the fire, somebody was retelling a thing that had come up out of the archive that spring ❧ a news clip decoded off a dead drive, from the last winter the news still came: a Dutch woman far above the tree line who had given up heat years before the world did, standing barefoot in the snow outside her cold camp, telling the reporter she had never been warmer. People walked days to join her, the clip said. The clip was eleven years old. There was no way left in the world to ask the north how it had gone.

Yara fed a knot of driftwood into the embers and listened the way you listen to weather on someone else's coast.

"Huh," she said.

The postcards had stopped the year the mail did. There was a small stack of them somewhere in a drawer in Amsterdam, or there wasn't; a stack of ever-colder places in a hand that never hurried. She did not claim the woman in the clip. She did not correct a word of it. The north kept what it kept.

Saga was retelling the handoff to Jef with both hands. Torbe had relocated into the warm hollow behind Yara's knees and gone to sleep with the totality of a dog who has never once doubted his arrangements. The stones were still giving back the day through her bare feet, and down the coast the dark water moved, and above the silhouette of La Gomera the last of the light had gone wherever it goes.

Yara breathed in through her nose. Slow. All the way, until there was no more room.

She held it at the top.

Then she let it down, all the way to the bottom, past where her mother had ever gone, and what was down there was warm.

A few words for the curious

  • lavvu: the Sámi herders' conical tent. Solveig rented several and called them yurts. The herders noticed, and said nothing, and raised the rent.
  • poi: weights on cords, swung in circles and figures ❧ LED pins for practice and small hands, fitted with wicks and lit for the real thing. Saga has been working the patterns since she was eight.
  • Torbellino [tor-beh-YEE-noh], "Torbe": whirlwind. La Tropa's smallest performer ❧ an electric-blue Pomeranian in a ruff and epaulettes who launches out of Yara's backpack mid-act, came from a fashion house, and does not miss it.
  • Callao Salvaje [kah-YAO sahl-VAH-heh]: a real town on the southwest coast ❧ a plaza with a lidded well and a lime tree, a circuit stop, and the kind of stones that hold the day's warmth well after dark.
