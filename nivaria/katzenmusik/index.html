Episode 20

Katzenmusik

The old Bienestar site, on the headland above Playa de Diego Hernández. October, 2041 ~~ fire night.

Welcome to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Caitlyn Meeks. Thanks for being here.

Saga had thirty-one names by sunset, and she had paid for them the only way she ever paid for anything, which was attention.

That was the job. Two hours before a show she went through the gathering crowd being thirteen and interested, which is the best disguise ever invented because it is not one, and she asked the questions nobody guards against. Whose baby is that. Who taught you to mend a net like that. Who are the figs for. People will hold the door of their whole lives open for a girl who asks about the figs. By the time the light went gold she had a fisherman up from La Caleta whose grandfather had painted his boat three colors because he couldn't choose, and a woman from the cave houses who had buried two husbands and outlived three doctors' opinions, and Doña Pino, down from Callao Salvaje with the Hidalgo cousins and a basket of the last figs of the year, who did not count because Doña Pino was not information. Doña Pino was infrastructure.

The names went into the notebook, and the notebook went to the tarot table, and that was the entire secret of the psychic act: there is no trick. People tell you who they are. They tell you all day long, to anyone who listens like it matters. And when the cards said your name back to you ~~ your actual name, said right, with the accent your grandmother put on it ~~ what you felt was not fooled. What you felt was found.

The fire nights had lived down on the beach once. Saga had heard the stories ~~ the brazier, the bass rolling off the water, a rabbit made of stars. But a crowd grows, and the barranco path has strong opinions after dark, and one year the fire simply walked up the hill and stayed. Nobody decided it. The old Bienestar was there, the way ruins are there, and it turned out that what a half-built wellness resort wants to be, more than anything in the world, is an amphitheater.

The great unfinished swimming pool of the Playa del Bienestar Wellness Thalasso had waited twenty-one years for water that was never coming, and had made other arrangements. Its shallow end held the stage. Its deep end held the crowd, on blankets and fish crates and two church pews nobody asked about. The concrete gave back the day's heat through the blankets like a big animal sleeping, and the sunset came straight in through the ground floor of the shell behind it, three stories of poured gray with no glass and no doors, room after room of clean ocean light, and the rebar on the unfinished roofline had rusted into a scribble that looked, at this hour, like handwriting the building was still working on.

Gertie had spent the whole afternoon on the wall above the deep end. Maisie Ardilla stood there now, ten meters of squirrel in a too-big hat, magnifying glass raised toward La Gomera as if the sunset were a clue. The paint was so fresh Saga could smell it under the woodsmoke, and Gertie stood in front of it with a tin cup, splattered to the elbows, receiving compliments the way other people receive weather.

Jef had strung the fairy lights through the rebar whiskers along the pool rim, and Auxi held the ladder and considered this a co-signature. Up the slope, in the sand traps of the dead golf course, the jaira goats had folded themselves down for the night like laundry. Ruymán sat on the stage edge tuning the timple, which he had been doing for forty minutes, which meant it had been in tune for thirty-nine. Torbe worked the front rows in his ruff, electric blue, seventeen years old and vibrating like a puppy, taking tribute.

And two kilometers up the dark slope, small as a struck match, one window burned in a white house that nobody looked at.

Saga looked at it. Looking was her job too.

The dark came up like a tide and Mateo met it with the low stuff, the bass you feel in the concrete before you agree you're hearing it, and the fire night began the way it always began, which is to say nobody could have told you the moment it did.

Saga delivered her notebook to the tarot table and stayed close, because watching the names go home was the part of the job she would have done for free. The fisherman with the three-color boat sat down across from Yara, holding his cap like it might testify against him. Yara laid the first card and said his name ~~ just his name, no flourish, the way you hand a man his own key ~~ and Saga watched his face do the thing she never got tired of. The small opening. The you know me.

That was what the act sold. It cost thirty-one questions about figs and it was worth more than it cost and everybody went away richer. Find the scam in that. People had tried.

When the last of the light was gone off La Gomera, Ruymán stopped tuning, which was the signal. The timple came up through Mateo's drone, folías first, the old strings threading the new low sound the way a needle threads a sail, and Yara walked out onto the shallow-end stage with the chains coiled in her hands and Torbe's backpack parked at the pool rim with Torbe's head sticking out of it, blue and patient, waiting on his cue like a professional.

She lit the poi from the brazier, one and then the other, unhurried, the way she did everything, and began.

There are no good sentences about Yara spinning fire. Saga had heard adults try. What she knew was this: the chains went around, and the fire drew its circles and figure-eights and slow orange ribbons on the dark, and somewhere in the first minute the crowd stopped being three hundred people and became one warm animal breathing, and watching it did something to the inside of your head that you had not asked it to do and did not mind. The concrete held the heat. The timple held the drone. The tall woman held the fire, and the night organized itself around her the way iron filings organize around the thing they have been waiting for.

It was somewhere in the second song that the headlights came down the dead road.

Small headlights. Close together, like the eyes of something modest. The access road to the Bienestar had a chain across it that had rusted open in 2029 and a surface the island had been taking back for twenty years, and the car came down it at the speed of a man reading his own upholstery, and it made the sound a solar Fiat 500 makes, which is the sound of a sewing machine with regrets.

The crowd had time to develop theories. The car parked itself at the top of the pool steps, at an angle that meant nothing to anyone who had never owned a parking space, and the firelight found the long scratched letters across the hood, old scars gone the color of the metal under them, and the door opened, and a man got out.

He was tall and old and ironed. That was the first thing Saga saw, that he had been ironed ~~ trousers with a crease, a collared shirt the color of sand, ironed for an occasion that had retired before she was born. He held a laminated sheet the way the priest in Garachico held the big book. He stood a moment in the light of a fire he had not been invited to, on the deck of a resort that did not exist, and three hundred people looked up at him, warm and fed and raised on puppet shows, and being an audience, they did the kindest possible thing.

They made him part of it. Somebody clapped.

And Tibor came up out of the deep end like a whale surfacing under a rowboat, glasses pushed up somewhere in the gray dreadlocks, arms already open, and his voice when it came had a joy in it so total that there was nothing anywhere for it to bounce off.

"Neighbor!" he said. "Twenty-four years! You came!"

He said it the way you'd mark an anniversary. Something they had managed together, at last.

"There are papas," Tibor said. "The little wrinkled ones, with the green mojo. Doña Pino brought figs."

"I am not here to eat," the man said.

"No, no," Tibor agreed, warmly, and waited, in the manner of a man for whom the papas remained on the table as an option.

❦

Saga knew who he was by then. Everybody did, in the loose way you know a landmark. This was the neighbor ~~ Tibor's word, said the way other people say the weather ~~ the man from the white house with the one lit window, who owned, on paper of a kind nobody printed anymore, the golf course the goats slept on and the beach the cave houses opened onto and the concrete bowl three hundred people were currently keeping warm.

She had just never seen him down here. Nobody had. He was a pair of binocular flashes on a far terrace at golden hour. He was a story Stef told with the good ending, the one about the irrigation line. He was two kilometers of uphill, made of years.

And now he stood at the top of the pool steps and raised the laminated sheet, and read from it.

"This," he said, "is an active development site."

He said it into the music, so it reached perhaps eleven people. Mateo, being a professional, kept the drone exactly where it was. The fire went around behind him, patient as a lighthouse.

"Construction will resume." He did not say when. Saga had the feeling the sheet said when, and that he had decided, at some final rank of privacy, not to share it. "The site must be kept clear. These modifications" ~~ the laminate traveled, taking in the fairy lights, the church pews, ten meters of fresh squirrel ~~ "are unauthorized."

"Unauthorized!" Gertie agreed from the dark, delighted, the way you'd shout bravo.

A woman near Saga said, appreciatively, that he was very good. Somebody passed him up a cup of nothing anybody had asked him if he wanted, and he held it, ironed and lit orange, a man standing in the applause of people he had come to evict, discovering that there was no surface anywhere on the night that his sentence would stick to.

He had more. Saga could see that he had more, the way you can see a wave still coming.

Then he stopped, because he had seen the fire.

Not the fire. The spinner. Saga watched it happen ~~ watched his eyes leave the laminate and find the tall woman in the shallow end with the burning chains writing on the dark, and stop there, and stay. His face did nothing at all. It was the loudest nothing Saga had ever watched a face do.

The fire went around. The timple climbed a little, working, unaware.

"Jürgen," he said. "Tell them the pool deck is not load-rated."

It was not a loud word. It was not even aimed like a stone, which was the strangeness Saga kept turning over afterward ~~ he said it flat, administrative, a line off a form, the way you'd say a docket number, and he said it toward the stage and stood waiting, the way a man waits who believes he has delegated something.

Saga knew every name on this coast. Names were her trade; she had thirty-one of them in her notebook from that evening alone, and none of them was Jürgen, and nobody in four years of working these crowds had ever been Jürgen, and she was already turning, out of habit, to find the who of it ~~ and then she followed the line of where he had said it, and stopped turning.

Yara's shoulders took one long breath. In over four counts. Held ~~ the way she taught it on the sand, hands flat on your knees, the breath is the one thing they can't schedule. Then the fire came around, unhurried, and kept going.

That was all. The chains did not change their arithmetic. The ribbons closed where ribbons close. Saga had once seen Yara catch a dropped club mid-pattern without her face admitting that anything had happened, and this was less than that. This did not require catching.

The name lay on the concrete where it had landed. Nobody picked it up.

There was nobody standing where it fell.

❦

The timple hit the gallop, which was Torbe's cue, and Torbe took his cue, because Torbe had never missed a cue in his professional life.

He came out of the backpack like a cork out of cider, seventeen years old and electric blue, ruff up, ears up, tail going like a pompom in a stiff wind, and did his circuit of the pool rim to the exact roar he had come for. The crowd loved Torbe the way crowds love the smallest performer, which is completely. He took the applause at a trot, past the fairy lights, past the pews, up along the rim ~~

~~ and stopped in front of the ironed man.

He looked up at him for a long moment. The man looked down at the blue dog and did not know him.

Torbe barked once. It was a small bark, and it was complete. It had a beginning, an argument, and a conclusion. Then he turned, with the full ceremony available to a Pomeranian in an Elizabethan ruff, and showed the man his pompom, and trotted back down into the light and the love.

The applause came in like the tide, and it was for the dog, and for the fire, and for the night, and it was not against the man at the top of the steps. It was worse than against him. It went around him, the way water goes around a piling, and closed warm on the other side, and left him standing in it holding a cup he had not asked for, under ten meters of squirrel, on the deck of the future, with his notice laminated against weather that was never going to touch it.

He said one word then, in German, quietly, to nobody.

"Katzenmusik."

Saga was close enough to hear it. She kept it, the way she kept things.

Tibor walked him back to the car. Nobody asked Tibor to do this; it would not have occurred to anybody to ask, or to him not to. He walked the neighbor out the way you walk out a guest, one big hand hovering an inch off the ironed shoulder, never landing, and he talked the whole way, warmly, about the pool.

"You built a good bowl, neighbor," Saga heard him say. "The best fire bowl on the coast. It holds the wind off. Everyone says so. This is not nothing, to build the best of a thing."

The man got into the little car without answering. The door made its tin sound.

"Come for the finale next time," Tibor said to the closed window, with perfect sincerity. "There are always papas."

The Fiat started, sewing-machine dignified, and swung itself around, and took the dead road up the headland the way it had come down, at the speed of a man who had stopped being expected anywhere years ago. The road climbed in switchbacks. At every turn he touched the brakes, and when he touched the brakes, the brake lights lit, and the long word scratched across the back of the car came up red out of the dark ~~ lit, and gone, lit, and gone, turn after turn, all the way up the hill, like something the car had decided to keep saying for him.

Saga could not read German. She watched the red word go up the mountain in flashes, patient as a lighthouse, until the one lit window took him back.

Up in the sand traps, a goat put its head up, considered the coast, and put it down again.

❦

The finale was the finale. Tibor came down into the shallow end with his clubs, the frosted ones he builds himself with a little sealed heart of circuitry in each, and turned them loose to drift through their colors, a slow aurora he could throw, and Yara answered him with the true fire, and for a while the two of them filled the bowl with it ~~ the invented light and the older kind, arguing gently, agreeing completely ~~ and the crowd made the sound crowds have made around fire since before there were crowds. The concrete gave back the day's heat. The fairy lights did their small faithful work in the rebar. Ten meters overhead, Maisie Ardilla raised her magnifying glass toward whatever the night was hiding, which is a detective's way of saying it will keep.

Afterward the night came down slowly, the way good ones do. Blankets folded. The Hidalgo cousins stacked the pews with the seriousness of pallbearers being paid in being seen. Doña Pino's basket went home lighter by every fig. Auxi was asleep before she finished claiming she wasn't, and was carried.

Saga sat on the warm concrete with her notebook and put the night away.

She wrote Katzenmusik, spelled the way it had sounded, because it had been said to everyone and belonged to anyone who caught it. Under it she wrote about the goat, and the cup nobody asked him if he wanted, and Tibor saying this is not nothing, to build the best of a thing, which she suspected was going to turn out to be about more than the pool, the way Tibor's sentences did.

Then she set the pen against the page to write the other word. The name that had landed on nothing.

She looked at it a while, the empty line.

She did not write it. Some things you keep by writing them down. Some things you keep by leaving the line empty and remembering why. She was thirteen, and she could already tell the difference, which is further along than most of us ever get.

The closer was hers now, officially, and she did it for the twenty or thirty still awake ~~ the small chains with the candle cups, little circles, the goodnight piece, fire scaled down to the size of a kept promise. And somewhere in the second minute she noticed her own breathing. In over four. Held for seven. Out over eight, slow, the way Yara taught it on the sand with her hands flat on her knees ~~ and she was three rounds in before she noticed she was doing them at all, because the rounds had arrived on their own, the way your name arrives when somebody says it right. Hers, without asking.

Across the deck, on the pool steps, Yara sat with Torbe asleep against her hip, blue and ancient and entirely satisfied with his evening, and watched her do it.

She saw Saga notice. Saga saw her see.

Neither of them said anything, and it was the whole conversation, and it was enough. The candles went around, small and sure, drawing their circles on the dark, and up the hill the white house kept its one window lit a while longer, and then it didn't, and the island held everybody, the way it does.

A few words for the curious

German this time, mostly, and one piece of resort Latin.

  • Katzenmusik: German, literally cat music. The old word for a rough serenade banged out under the window of somebody the town had opinions about; in one white house above the Adeje coast, the word for any music made by people under forty with amplifiers. A word for music used only ever by people the music was not for.
  • Thalasso: resort Greek for seawater wellness. The Bienestar's plan was to pump the Atlantic eighty meters up a cliff, warm it, filter it, scent it, and sell it back to people who had an ocean in front of them the whole time. The pool is doing better work now, and holds the wind off the fire, and this is not nothing.
  • Papas arrugadas: the little wrinkled potatoes, boiled in seawater until they salt-crust, eaten skins and all with green mojo. On this island, arriving with them is a recognized form of diplomacy, and offering them twice is a whole foreign policy.
  • Load-rated: engineer's term for the weight a structure is signed off to carry. For the record, a meter of 2020 concrete carries a fire night without noticing. The sheet was not wrong. It was only irrelevant, which was the sheet's whole evening.
