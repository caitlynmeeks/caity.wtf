The Hold Is the Loaf

The Cebolla sisters' finca, and the old camino real down to Garachico. Early November, 2041 ⁓ fledging season.

Welcome back to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Caitlyn Meeks. Thanks for being here.

The Viscount came for her an hour before dawn, which had never once happened before.

He arrived the way he arrived everywhere, which was already there. Saga came up out of sleep sure that somebody was looking at her, and somebody was: the black cat with the limp, sitting on the sill of the round window outside the collected pieces of stained glass, looking in at her the way you look at a person who is late.

His name was long, and he had earned it by hand.

If you walked the water channel above Querencia at any hour of any day, you would find him at one particular bend of it, the one where the water comes round a stone and picks up speed: sitting bolt upright with his tail curled over his feet, watching the water go by, and every so often putting one paw into it. Not to drink. To stop it. He had been doing this since before Saga could remember. He did it with tremendous seriousness and total concentration, the way cats do it at a running tap, and the water went on going past, and he flicked his paw dry, and considered, and did it again.

He had never caught the water. He had never stopped trying. And at some point Remedios had watched him at it long enough to conclude that a devotion like that ought to come with land, and she had given him the channel, and a name to go with it: el Vizconde de la Acequia Alta. The Viscount of the Upper Water Channel.

It is an enormous title for a stone gutter eleven inches wide, and he took it without any sign of surprise. Everyone on the mountain used it. Everyone on the mountain also kept the protocol that came with it, which Saga had learned when she was nine and had never once been tempted to test: the Viscount is not picked up. The Viscount is not called. The Viscount arrives, and is received, and precedes you through doors.

He said one word through the glass. Not a meow. A syllable, flat and consonantal, like a small door shutting.

Saga looked at him through a piece of amber glass. He looked back at her through the same piece, which turned him gold. Then he turned his head and looked up the mountain, and held it, and looked back at her.

"Okay," she said, to a cat, at five in the morning. "Okay, okay."

She went down the ladder in her sweater and her mother's old boots, and Sirius rose from his croissant and fell in without being invited, because a cat walking somewhere with purpose in the dark is a matter for the whole committee.

The Viscount led. Up through the wet terraces with his tail carried high and his bad back foot touching down light and quick, along the water channel that was his entire jurisdiction and his whole name, under laurels that let go of the night's fog one drop at a time, plick, and then plick, onto the moss. It was that hour when the world is charcoal and blue and every sound arrives with its edges sharpened. Saga could hear the channel running. She could hear Sirius's nails. She could hear her own boots, and a bell somewhere far off that was Perenquén going over a wall he now had a gate for, out of pure principle.

And then, from up the mountain, somebody laughed at her.

Saga stopped dead in the path.

It came down through the trees from the direction of the sisters' finca. It was a huge sound, wheezing and broken, going up at the end like a question, halfway between a donkey and a person crying and somebody laughing so hard they can't get their breath. It went on too long. It cracked in the middle and started again. Every hair on Saga's arms stood up.

Sirius pressed against her leg, warm and solid, and she put a hand down into his ruff and held on to it.

The Viscount did not even turn his head. He went on up the path at exactly the same pace as before, and Saga stood where she was a moment longer, wishing hard that he had been frightened too, because then she would have known what to do with her own.

The gate of the finca stood open, and every window in the house was lit, at five in the morning, and the front door was blocked by a mattress.

Saga stood in the courtyard and read the evidence like a page. The mattress was standing on its edge across the doorway, held there by a wheelbarrow and a sack of feed. Behind it the kitchen shutters had been closed, but light came out around them in bright seams. A ladder lay flat on the ground, which was wrong ⁓ ladders on that farm lived vertically, against things. There was soot. There were, unmistakably, three separate handprints of soot on the whitewashed wall by the door, all of them large, and one enormous smear at knee height that had a hoof in it.

Then the laugh went off again, inside the house, and something metal fell over, and eleven cats came out through the rubber flap at once like a single dark liquid and reassembled themselves on the wall, all facing the door, in a state of religious excitement.

The mattress moved aside eight inches. Angustias's face appeared in the gap. Her hair, always pinned with whatever was nearest, was pinned with a wooden spoon. Her eyebrows were grey with soot and her expression was that of a woman having the best morning of her recent life.

"You." she said. "Excellent. Take this."

Saga was handed a candle lantern through the gap in the mattress.

"Now the rule," said Angustias. "Are you listening? This is the only rule. Do not let her see it."

"Wait. What?"

"The light."

"Which light?"

"Any light," said Angustias, and pulled her in through the gap.

The kitchen was freezing, and it smelled of cold ash, and everything in it was in the wrong place.

Every stick of furniture was pushed to the walls. The long table was on its side. A bedsheet hung from the beams across the middle of the room like a sail, and behind the sail, the fire was out and the hearth was a black disaster, ash blown two meters across the tiles in a fan, with a clear trail through it where something had skidded. A young goat with a splinted leg who was not supposed to be a kitchen animal and was, stood in the corner wearing a fine grey coat of ash and a look of total, uncomplaining betrayal. Remedios was on her knees at the dresser with her cheek flat to the floor and one arm underneath it up to the shoulder, in her nightgown, blackened with soot. Milagros stood in the middle of the room holding a folded towel and doing nothing whatsoever with it, barefoot in the ash, with clean feet.

"Doña," Remedios was saying to the underside of the dresser, "le juro que esto no es personal." Madam, I swear to you, this is nothing personal.

From under the dresser came a sound like a rusty pump handle being worked by a lunatic.

"What is it?" said Saga, with her whole heart.

"She came down the chimney at four," said Angustias, taking up a position at the left flank with a colander in her hand, for reasons that would never be explained. "Straight into the hearth, into the hot ash, in a cloud, like the devil arriving. Sat up. Looked at us. Laughed. Went behind the flour. Came out. Went under there." She pointed. "We are in hour one."

"But what is it?"

Three women said "una pardela" at the same instant, in three different tones ⁓ Remedios's flat, from the floor; Angustias's delighted, from the flank; Milagros's not really addressed to Saga at all ⁓ and then all three of them, without discussion, went still and looked at each other over the wreckage of their kitchen. Something passed between them that Saga had no name for yet. It lasted about a second.

And if you have never met one: a pardela is a Cory's shearwater, and she is what a Canarian night in autumn sounds like, whether or not you ever learned her name. She is a true seabird. She lives out over the open Atlantic and comes to land for one reason only, which is to raise a single chick in a burrow in a cliff, in the dark, and then she goes back out. She is known here above all for the noise, that broken laughing wail rolling off the cliffs after sunset, which has been unsettling visitors and comforting everybody else for a very long time.

Her children have one great difficulty. They leave the burrow in autumn, at night, alone, having never flown, and they steer by the shine of the sea ⁓ and they will go to any bright thing, and a bright thing over land is a trap, because a shearwater who lands flat cannot get up again. Which is why, for a good many years now, this island has spent its Octobers sending people out into the dark to pick up the ones that fell.

"Bueno," said Remedios, to the dresser. "Vamos."

What followed was the single most competent shambles Saga had ever been part of.

Remedios directed it from the floor with her arm still under the dresser, in imperatives and measurements. The bedsheet, it turned out, was a wall: it existed to make the room smaller, then smaller again, and Angustias moved it in stages with two chairs and language. Saga's job was the lantern, and the lantern's job was to be behind the bird, always ⁓ "she runs from it, she runs from it, so put it where you don't want her" ⁓ and Saga finally understood that she was not holding a light, she was holding a fence. The Viscount supervised from the top of the dresser. The cats supervised from the window. Sirius stayed in the doorway with his shoulder against the mattress, which nobody had asked him to do, and which was the only thing holding it up.

The bird came out from under the dresser like a shot of grey water, ran along the wall with her wings half open, hit the sheet, ran back, disappeared behind the woodbox, laughed at everybody from in there, took a run at the ash fan and skidded, went under the sink, came out of the sink cupboard with a wooden spoon she had no use for, dropped it, laughed again, and reversed into a corner made of a bedsheet, a colander, a candle lantern, and a woman in a nightgown.

"AHORA," said Remedios.

Milagros put the towel over her.

The entire kitchen stopped. Everything stopped. The bundle under the towel was very small and completely still, and it did not laugh, and in the silence Saga could hear the ash settling and eleven cats breathing and her own heart going like a fist on a door.

"Ay," said Angustias softly, into the quiet. "Ay, mi niña. Ya está." There now. That's it. It's done.

Remedios came up off the floor with her sooty knees creaking and washed her hands ⁓ twice, the second time up to the elbow, without hurrying, while everyone waited ⁓ and dried them, and warmed them at the stove, and only then took the bundle.

"The hold," she said, "is the loaf."

The loaf-hold is this: you come down over the wings with both hands and you gather the bird the way you gather a warm loaf you don't want to dent, wings pinned kindly to the body, and you keep your thumbs where you can count them. The towel came away and the pardela rose out of it in Remedios's hands like bread out of a basket ⁓ sooty, furious, compact, magnificent ⁓ and Saga saw her properly for the first time.

She was grey-brown on top and white underneath, and she was bigger than Saga had expected, about the size of a gull, with a soft heavy head. Her eye was very dark and very round. Her beak was long and pale and hooked at the end, and along the top of it, laid on like something added afterward, was a small raised tube.

"That's for the salt," said Remedios, seeing where Saga was looking. "She drinks seawater. Her whole life. That gets rid of it." She turned the bird a little. "And she smells with it. Better than the dog. She can smell fish from kilometers away, in the dark, in the middle of the ocean, and that is how she knows where anything is."

She opened one wing a little way, and then further, and then all the way, and Saga said "oh," out loud, the small soft noise you make when somebody shows you a baby.

The wing kept going. It went past the edge of the table. It was longer than Saga's whole arm and narrow as a plank, and Remedios held it open for a moment so she could see it properly, the feathers laid over each other like tiles on a roof, and then folded it away again the way you'd close a fan.

"Hold your hands out," said Remedios.

Saga held her hands out and Remedios put the bird into them, and Saga's first thought, immediately and completely, was that she had been handed an empty coat.

She weighed nothing. All that bird ⁓ the wing that crossed the table, the head, the terrible beak ⁓ and she weighed less than the cat, less than a bag of flour, less than a chicken. There was almost nothing there. It was like holding warm bread. She could feel a heart going very fast against her palm, and hollow bones under the feathers, and the whole animal shifting slightly to keep her balance, and Saga stood absolutely still in a sooty kitchen and did not breathe.

"She's so light," she whispered.

"Mm," said Remedios. "She has to be. She doesn't come to land."

"Ever?"

"To be born," said Remedios. "And to have a chick, later, if she's lucky. That's all. Everything else happens out there." She said it plainly, the way you'd say a neighbour lives in Icod. "This one has never been on land in her life except the hole she hatched in. She came out of it three nights ago, in the dark, on her own, and she has never flown before, and she was going to the water. And she saw our window."

Saga looked down at the bird in her hands.

The bird looked back up at her, and opened her beak, and laughed in her face, and Saga laughed too, because there was nothing else you could do about it.

She smelled like the sea. Not like fish, like the sea itself ⁓ cold and clean and enormous, three kilometers downhill and somehow standing in a kitchen full of onions.

"Keep your thumbs where you can count them," said Remedios. "That beak is a stapler."

"She's beautiful," said Saga.

"She is," said Angustias, coming in fast over Saga's shoulder to see, so close that Saga could feel her breath. "Look at that nose. Look at it. I want to touch the little pipe ⁓ "

"Angustias."

"One touch ⁓ "

The stapler closed on her thumb.

Angustias's whole long body did the entire shout except the sound. She rose up on her toes, and her eyes went enormous, and her free hand grabbed the back of a chair, and not one bit of noise came out of her, because there was a wild bird in the room. She looked down at her thumb. The bird looked back up at her with the thumb still in her beak, and did not let go.

"Ay," said Angustias, in a small voice. "Ay, ay, ay."

Milagros got the thumb back by putting one finger on the bird's throat, gently, the way you'd quiet a string. Remedios came over and looked at the damage, and wrapped it in a strip of clean rag, and then lifted the wrapped hand and kissed it, twice, on the knuckle above the hurt.

"Better," said Angustias, surprised, and flexed it, and it was.

Angustias and Saga put the long table back on its feet, and somebody lit the lamp over it, and the kitchen stopped being a wreck and became a clinic.

They looked at the wings from three sides at once, heads together over the wood, close enough that Saga could smell soap and soot and onions. Angustias read the folded wing the way she read a plank, along the grain, hands off, her face about a hand's width from the feathers. Remedios worked the joints with two fingers, and talked to the bird the whole time.

"Perdóneme, señorita. Ya sé, ya sé. Un momentito nada más." Forgive me, miss. I know, I know. Just one small moment.

Every so often she blew very softly on the back of the bird's neck. Saga didn't understand why until she saw the bird's eyes go half shut.

"She likes that," said Saga.

"Everyone likes that," said Remedios.

Milagros didn't touch her at all. She had come around to the head end and simply crouched there with her chin on the table, eye to eye with the pardela, at a distance of about a hand, the two of them regarding each other with total seriousness while the others worked, like two people in a waiting room who have decided not to make conversation and are perfectly happy about it.

"Nothing broken," said Remedios, at the shoulder.

"Nothing bent," said Angustias, at the tip.

"Thursday," whispered Milagros, to the bird.

The other two straightened, and everybody looked at the window, where the afternoon was flat and grey and doing nothing whatsoever.

"Three nights," Milagros whispered, "and the third one has the wind."

Remedios straightened up and put both hands in the small of her back. "There is nothing wrong with you, señorita," she said, "except geography. And you're hungry."

Saga went out to the shed with Angustias to fetch a box for her, and that was where the morning turned over.

The boxes were on the top shelf: flat-folded cardboard, a stack of them as deep as Saga's forearm was long, furred grey with dust. Angustias took the top one down and shook it open, and Saga saw the printing on the side. Old red ink, stencilled, the same on every one of them. CAMPAÑA DE LA PARDELA.

"What's the campaign?"

Angustias looked at the stack for a second. Then she sat down on an upturned crate, which she did not usually do, and told her.

"Every October the young ones come out of their holes and go to the sea. They go at night. They have never flown and nobody teaches them; they just go, and they follow the light off the water, because for a million years the brightest thing at night was the sea." She turned the flat box over in her hands. "And then we put lights everywhere. Roads. Hotels. The football ground at Icod. All of it brighter than the water."

"So they went to the lights," said Saga.

"They went to the lights, and the lights are on poles in the middle of towns, and they cannot take off from flat ground." She said it without any drama at all. "So every October the island went out and picked them up off the roads. Schoolchildren. Grandmothers. The police, sometimes. You kept a box like this in the house from September, because you were going to need it. You would find one under a car, or on a roundabout, or walking down the middle of the road at two in the morning with no idea in the world how it got there. You put it in the box, and you called the number, and somebody drove down and let it go over the water."

"What number?"

"Nine hundred, two eight two, two two eight," sang Angustias, without a pause of any kind, and then stopped, and looked at her own hands.

Saga did not say anything.

"Three thousand birds a year, some years," Angustias went on, after a moment. "For the whole island. And do you know where the worst of it was? Not up here. Down south. Adeje, Arona, all along that coast ⁓ the hotels, the promenades, the golf." She paused, and her mouth went flat. "El Hotel Hard Rock."

"Ay, madre mía," said Remedios and Milagros, at exactly the same moment, in exactly the same tone, without either of them looking up.

They had clearly been doing this for years. Saga made a small sound into her sleeve.

"Hundreds out of Adeje alone, every single October," said Angustias. "You would stand in a car park down there at midnight, under all that light, and they would come down around you out of the dark like somebody up there was dropping them."

"And you went down?"

"We went down. Us three and a van, every October, for twenty-five years." She handed Saga the open box. "One year we took ninety-one out of the streets behind those hotels in three nights. Ninety-one. And that was us being one van out of I don't know how many."

Saga looked at the stack of boxes on the shelf. She was very good at arithmetic and she did not need to be.

She thought about the lantern in her hand, and how it had worked as a fence. She thought about a bird coming down a chimney at four o'clock in the morning because three women were awake with a lamp lit, and she thought about the mountain she had walked up in the dark that morning, all the way from her own house to this one, with nothing burning anywhere on it at all.

She got as far as opening her mouth.

"North wind tonight," said Angustias, standing up off the crate, and she put the shed door to behind them, gently, the way you close a door on somebody sleeping.

And that was all either of them said about the boxes.

Hungry meant fish, and fish meant the coast, and the coast was three kilometers down and a whole economy away, and this is where the mountain did the thing the mountain does. Remedios stood at the shelf where the mesh unit lived ⁓ a scavenged tablet in a pallet-wood frame with a rind of candle wax on one corner ⁓ and typed with one finger, at dictation speed, a message to the Martín family down at the harbor: PARDELA EN CASA. SARDINES PORFA. SMALL UGLIES R FINE.

The answer came back before the kettle did. PUT ASIDE 4 U ALREADY.

Then, a moment later, from a different Martín on the same unit: ONE CAME DOWN? AFTER ALL THESE YEARS?

And then, from a third: MAMA WANTS TO KNOW IF ITS BIG.

The sardines came up the mountain the way everything came up the mountain, which is to say by relay, and with commentary. A Martín kid ran them from the harbor to the crossroads. The crossroads passed them to a man with a mule and a forecast. The mule declined the last slope, on principle, so the sardines walked the final kilometer under the arm of a woman coming up anyway to trade eggs, who stayed forty minutes and left with an onion braid, three eggs richer than she'd arrived, having seen the bird and been laughed at personally. By dusk half the northwest knew the sisters had a pardela, the way the northwest knew anything: completely, and by nobody in particular.

The Viscount sat by the kitchen step throughout, and when the sardines were unwrapped Remedios brought him the heads on a saucer, one at a time, and set the saucer down and stepped back before he ate, which is protocol.

"Señor Vizconde," she said.

He received them with his eyes half closed.

Not like charity. Like salary.

Feeding a pardela is not feeding a hen. Remedios held her loaf-wise; Saga offered slivers of sardine down the stapler at an angle Remedios called "the respectful diagonal"; and the bird ate the way the sea takes things ⁓ entirely, at once, with a small violence that had nothing personal in it, and then it looked around for the rest of the ocean, and laughed.

Out in the yard, at dusk, two bells crossed the courtyard through the goat-height gate in the shared wall, and then both, going somewhere unhurried together, and nobody remarked on it, because some victories are just the weather now.

In the afternoon the bird slept, and the sisters went out to the terraces to plant, and Saga was left alone in the house with instructions to shout if the box moved.

The box did not move. So Saga did what she was built to do, which was look at things.

She had never once been in the sisters' kitchen alone, and a house alone tells you things it will not say in company. She went slowly, with her hands behind her back, the way you walk in a church you don't belong to, and within ten minutes she had found three separate answers and lost every question she had.

The first was the doorframe.

It was the frame between the kitchen and the back passage, old pine gone amber, and it was covered in marks: little level scratches cut with a knife, each one with a date beside it, going up the wood like the rungs of a ladder. Height marks. Children's height marks. Saga's whole chest went off like a struck bell, because this was proof, this was a house where children had been measured, three little girls growing up against one doorframe, and she leaned in with her nose almost on the wood to read the years.

There were four columns.

She counted them twice. Four. And the dates in the first three ran together, the same years, three children growing up side by side, which was exactly what she wanted ⁓ except that all three columns started at the same height and the same year, and children do not do that unless they are the same age, and three women who all claim to be the youngest cannot all be the same age, because then the joke would not be a joke.

And the fourth column had no name and no dates and only two marks in it, one very low and one very high, and both of them had been cut with a different knife.

The second was the photograph.

It hung by the window in a frame Saga could have identified blindfolded ⁓ pallet pine, joined at the corners with no nails ⁓ and it showed three young women on a wall in the sun somewhere, laughing so hard that two of them were bent over, in clothes from the before. Saga went up close. The one on the left was Remedios; you could see the whole of her in the set of the shoulders. The one on the right was Angustias, unmistakable, hair like a rusted roof. The one in the middle had her face entirely hidden behind her own hands.

The middle one was wearing an apron. It was inside out.

Saga stood in front of that photograph for a long time and understood that Milagros had been standing in exactly that spot, hiding her face in exactly that way, for at least twenty years and probably longer, and that everyone in this photograph was in on it, and that whatever she was looking at was not a coincidence and was also not going to explain itself.

The third was the bedroom, and she only saw it because the door was open, and she only looked because she was already ruined.

There was one enormous bed in it, built to fit the room and clearly built by hand, and it had three pillows on it, and it was made.

Next to it was a single narrow cot with a folded blanket and one pillow, and it was also made.

Saga backed out of the doorway with the distinct feeling of having read someone's letter, and stood in the kitchen with her heart going, and got out the notebook.

She wrote: THEY ARE SISTERS ~ and stopped, and looked at it, and drew a line through it.

She wrote: THEY ARE NOT SISTERS ~ and looked at that for even longer, and drew a line through that too.

Then she wrote, in her best handwriting, because it was the only thing on the page she was actually sure of: 4 columns.

Outside, three voices were coming up the terrace path, arguing amiably about compost in the way that carries, and one of them was singing the tail of a sentence, and Saga shut the notebook fast, and was standing by the box looking innocent by the time they came in.

Then it got dark, and the house did the thing that Saga would remember longer than the bird.

It filled up.

They came in the way water finds a low place. Eleven cats, at least eleven, arriving through a rubber flap and over a windowsill and down from somewhere in the beams. A hen who was not supposed to be indoors and knew it and did it anyway, taking up a position under the table with the air of a delegate. The young goat with a splinted leg who lived in the kitchen for the duration watched from atop a bench. Sirius, who took one look at all this, decided it was fine, and lay down across the doorway like a rug with responsibilities.

Everything in that room had been broken once. Everything in that room had been kept.

Dinner was onions: a great blackened pan of them cooked down slow with garlic and fennel seed until they went sweet and dark and gave up entirely, poured over papas cooked in seawater, goat cheese crumbled on top. Green mojo so fresh it was still angry. Bread that Angustias tore rather than cut, and handed round in pieces, warm, still attached to each other until the last second.

Saga's plate arrived in front of her without her asking for it and without anyone appearing to have made it, which is how everything happened in that house. Then Remedios reached over and put a second piece of cheese on it, and then Angustias, passing behind her, put a piece of bread on it, and then Milagros, without looking up from her own plate, moved the good chair an inch closer to the fire with her foot, and Saga sat in the middle of all this being quietly, thoroughly fed by three women who never once said anything about it, and thought: oh.

This is what they're like when nobody needs saving.

The bird had a bowl of her own on the table. Nobody had suggested otherwise.

Afterward, Angustias produced a timple from behind a stack of seed trays, tuned it badly on purpose to annoy Remedios ⁓ it worked, immediately, and both of them enjoyed it enormously ⁓ and then played it well, because she could.

They started with a filthy old folía about a customs inspector, which was not at all the sort of thing Saga expected from women of that age. Remedios sang the low line of it with an absolutely straight face while Angustias's eyebrows did all the work above her, and Saga laughed so hard she had to put her fork down, and la Regenta stood in the middle of the floor on her splinted leg looking from one of them to the other.

Then Angustias's hand changed on the strings and the room changed with it, and Milagros began to sing.

Saga had never heard her make a sound above talking. She had spent a whole day deciding Milagros was the one who didn't sing.

The voice that came out was not a woman's voice and it was not a man's. It went up very high and stayed there, thin and clear and strange, sliding between the notes instead of stepping down them, and the words were in no language Saga could place ⁓ or they were in Spanish and simply arranged wrong, on purpose, the way you might arrange flowers in a boot. She sat in her chair by the fire in that big man's waistcoat with all its pockets, grey hair cropped close to her head, long hands loose on her knees, her eyes half shut, and she did not perform any of it. She just made the sound and let it go across the room.

And underneath it, without any signal Saga could catch, the other two came in under her ⁓ Remedios low and steady, Angustias high and full and openly operatic, the two of them building a floor for that strange voice to walk around on top of. They had obviously done this ten thousand times. They kept it quiet, and they kept it well behind her, and neither of them once looked at her while she sang.

Saga sat with a one-eyed dog's head on her knee and the fire going down and eleven cats asleep on every surface, and did not move a muscle, in case it stopped.

Two cats swatted briefly at each other on the dresser and were separated by Remedios without her interrupting the verse.

Later, the fire got low and the talk got slow, and Milagros took Saga's hair down out of its band without asking and re-braided it, comfortably, with cold clever fingers, the way an aunt does, and Saga ⁓ who was thirteen and did not permit this sort of thing at home anymore ⁓ permitted it completely, and stared into the fire, and did not write anything down.

Saga stayed that night. The mesh carried her goodnight down to Querencia and the mountain carried the rest, and Linnea's answer came back in full: OK. ~ and then, a minute later, because her mother had married into this island but was not from it: DO NOT LET IT BITE YOU, THEY BITE. Saga looked at Angustias's thumb, wrapped in a strip of clean rag and held slightly aloft all evening like a small flag of surrender, and typed back: too late but not me.

She was given the alcove bed under the stairs, which smelled of lavender and warm cat, and she lay in it listening to the house tick down, and she was almost gone when the box laughed.

Three in the morning is the pardela's hour. Nobody had told Saga this. The box sat in the corner of the kitchen with a cloth over it for the dark, and at three it went off like a haunted kettle ⁓ manic pardela laughter, up through the sleeping house, joy or grief or both at once in no proportion anyone could certify, a sound with saltwater all through it. Saga was up before she had decided to be. She wasn't the only one.

The kitchen assembled itself in nightclothes and candlelight: Remedios in a flannel gown ironed, somehow, even for sleeping; Angustias in a man's shirt and her boots, unlaced, one black and one brown even now, which answered a question Saga hadn't asked about whether the boots were a system; Milagros exactly as she always was, as if night were a place she simply hadn't been facing. Tea happened by the usual method of three women and no collisions. Nobody shushed the bird.

And then, through the open shutter, from high in the dark over the terraces, something answered it.

The call came down thin and wild from the black sky ⁓ the same laugh, farther away, moving, a young bird going out on the night wind toward the sea it had never touched and knew everything about. The box went silent for a moment, listening. Then it laughed back with everything it had, the candle flames leaning, the cloth trembling, and the two sounds hung in the dark over the onion terraces, one going, one grounded, and Saga stood in a borrowed blanket with her chest doing something enormous, and nobody explained anything to anybody.

"Ay," said Angustias, softly, when it faded.

"Yes," said Remedios.

Angustias looked at the covered box for a while. When she spoke, her commentary voice was gone; what was under it was quieter and had a grain to it, like the good side of a plank.

"That's how he laughed," she said. "Exactly like that."

Saga went very still, the way she went still at the tarot table when an adult forgot she was thirteen.

"Who?" she said.

The sisters looked at each other, and something went round the table that Saga felt more than saw, the way you feel a door open somewhere behind you in a house.

Then all three of them started talking at once.

This had not happened all week. They had poured for each other and passed things and finished each other's work without a word for four days, and now they were interrupting, contradicting, talking over the tops of each other's sentences like anyone's family anywhere, and Angustias got up to make more tea nobody had asked for, and Remedios laughed out loud twice in the first minute, which Saga had not heard her do at all.

His name was Linolada. He was Croatian. He came in the coliving years, when Querencia was forty dreamers and a water problem, and he could look at a broken pallet and see a cathedral in kit form.

"You sleep in his work, girl," said Angustias. "Your loft. Your mother's porch. Everybody on this mountain sleeps in his work or eats off it or leans on it and never thinks about it once."

"He never used a nail," said Remedios, and she put her cup down when she said it, so that it landed properly.

"Not one. Not ever. In his life." Angustias touched the carpenter's pencil behind her ear without appearing to know she'd done it. "Joinery. You cut the two pieces so they cannot imagine life apart."

"And he was handsome," said Remedios, into her tea, with a completely straight face.

"Qué guapo," said the other two, together, quietly, the way you say a response you have said a thousand times in the same place in the same story.

And then nobody said anything at all for a moment.

It was not a silence like the ones Saga knew. All three of them had gone somewhere, at the same time, without leaving the table. Remedios was looking at the middle of the table and not seeing it. Angustias had stopped pouring, with the pot still tilted. And Saga, thirteen and watching very carefully, understood that she was sitting in a kitchen with three women who were each remembering the same man in whatever way each of them remembered him, and that she was not going to be told any of it.

"The hair," said Angustias, coming back first. "Blond, down to here, in ropes. Filthy. He would tie it up with whatever was on the bench."

"The shoulders," said Milagros, from somewhere behind Saga.

"The hands," said Remedios. "Enormous. And he painted his nails, every colour there was, because the girls in the village painted them for him and he would not take it off. I have never in my life seen anything look so good on a man."

"He sang while he worked," said Remedios, "badly, and he flirted with everything that had a pulse, including the goats, including Father Ramiro, and he had a laugh that arrived before he did. You'd hear it come up the terraces and you had, oh, a minute. To put away anything fragile. To decide what you were going to pretend you'd been doing."

"The town," said Angustias, delighted, refilling everyone, "held certain views about this house."

"There was the morning," said Remedios, serenely, "that all three of us came out of his cabin at first light, wrecked, filthy, and half undressed, and were seen."

Saga's pencil was out. Nobody had seen her get it out.

"The three of us and him," said Remedios, to her tea. "All night. Every one of us."

"His hands," said Angustias.

"His hands," Remedios agreed.

"And what he had us doing with ours." Angustias turned her cup around by the handle. "By morning we were finished. Completely spent, the three of us."

"And the positions," said Milagros, from the dark end of the kitchen.

"The positions," said Angustias. "At my age. I did not know we were capable."

Saga's pencil had stopped moving. All three of them were looking somewhere else with tremendous innocence, and not one of them was looking at her, and it occurred to her, slowly, that she was being worked.

"The roof beam had cracked in the storm," said Angustias, taking pity, "and he glued it at midnight, and he had no clamps long enough. So we were the clamps."

She held her arms up over her head to show it, elbows locked, hands flat against a ceiling that wasn't there, and left them there while she talked, because that was the only way to tell it.

"Eight hours. Like this. You cannot put them down, not for a minute, or the two faces open up a hair and the whole joint is a lie forever. So it is four idiots in a cabin at two in the morning holding a roof up with their hands." Her arms came down. "And he fed us. With his own fingers, one at a time, going round and round the four of us, putting it straight into our mouths, warm ⁓ "

"Bread," said Remedios, into the pause. "He had been baking all evening. Warm bread, and soft cheese, because our hands were over our heads and we could not feed ourselves."

"Warm bread," Angustias agreed, with enormous satisfaction, "like baby birds," and drank her tea, and let Saga catch up.

"And he talked the entire night," said Remedios. "Terrible jokes. Filthy songs. Every story he had, and some of them twice. He knew that if we were laughing hard enough we would not notice our shoulders. He was right. And that is the reason that joint is still up there."

"Milagros fell asleep standing," said Remedios.

"Standing," Angustias agreed. "Arms up. Like a saint in a niche."

"I did not fall asleep," said Milagros. "I stopped attending."

"And at six," said Remedios, "the light came in the door sideways ⁓ that low first light, all the way across the floor ⁓ and he said bueno, and we let go, one at a time, very slowly, like it might come down. And it didn't. It hasn't." She turned her cup a quarter turn. "And then we could not put our arms down. Any of us. They just stayed up. And we stood there in that cabin, the four of us, arms in the air like we'd won something, laughing until we cried, at six o'clock in the morning."

"And that," said Angustias, "is when the Perdomo woman walked past the open door."

Saga made a sound.

"Yes," said Remedios placidly. "In the previous day's clothes. Sore. Barely able to lift our arms."

"He never used a nail," said Angustias, and picked up her tea. "He used us."

Remedios made a small sound into her cup that she did not let become a laugh.

"The town's version was better," said Milagros, from wherever she was standing, which was not where she had been standing.

"The town's version," said Remedios, "was much better. We never corrected it." She looked at the fire. "Correcting it would have ended something."

Angustias nodded slowly, and did not say anything, and looked sorry that the story was over.

"He wrote us a love letter once," said Angustias. "All three of us. The same letter."

"One letter," said Remedios.

"It was a cutting list," said Angustias, and drank her tea with enormous dignity, and did not say whether she still had it, and had it.

"There's a fourth column on the doorframe," said Saga.

She had not planned to say it. It came out of her the way things come out of you at three in the morning, and the moment it was out she knew she'd done something, because the kitchen changed temperature.

Angustias looked at Remedios. Remedios looked at her tea.

"Yes," she said.

"Two marks. Cut with a different knife."

"Yes."

"Whose are they?"

"His," said Remedios.

Saga waited. The fire ticked. The bird, under her cloth, said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than the laughing.

"But there's only two," said Saga, more quietly.

"The day he came," said Remedios, "and the day he went."

And Saga, who was thirteen and merciless and had never in her life left a mystery alone, opened her mouth to ask why one of them was so much lower than the other, and looked at the three of them sitting there in the candlelight, and closed it again.

That was the part that would have made her father proud, and he will never hear about it.

"Tell her the one with the goat," said Milagros, into the quiet, in the tone of a woman changing a subject on purpose and not caring who noticed.

"The goat one isn't ours," said Remedios, with real regret, the way you decline a dance you would have loved. "It belongs to her mother." She turned to Saga. "Ask her sometime about the winter of the goat."

Saga underlined something twice.

Saga sat in the middle of all this with her notebook open and her whole face lit up, and then she asked the enormous question, at three in the morning, the way you can only ask it at three in the morning.

"Are you actually sisters?"

The kitchen did not go quiet, exactly. It went attentive, the way the laurels go before rain.

"There is a certificate," said Remedios.

"Of what?"

"Yes," said Remedios.

"She asked the big one," said Angustias, to her tea, gently, "at three in the morning, in a borrowed blanket. The crowd is holding its breath."

Milagros crossed the kitchen without crossing the intervening floor, and put something in Saga's hand: a boiled sweet in waxed paper, amber, one of Anna's, from a waistcoat pocket that had held it for who knows what occasion, and it was warm from being near her, and she closed Saga's fingers over it with both of her long cool hands.

"You'll ask again when you're older," she said. "It'll be a better question then."

And then the box laughed ⁓ the whole mad joyous pump-handle of it, at the tea, at the hour, at certificates, at the entire concept of questions ⁓ and the kitchen came apart into laughing, all four of them, and the question dissolved the way fog dissolves, without a moment you could point to, leaving everything wet and bright where it had been.

On the second day they tested her in the yard, because Saga asked if they could, and the sisters believed in letting the mountain answer questions where possible.

It went the way a first pancake goes. The pardela stood on the flat courtyard flagstones, magnificently at sea level in her own mind, and ran, and beat her long thin perfect wings, and got about as high as a bucket, and came down again on her big pink webbed feet ⁓ which are set so far back on her that on land she cannot really walk, only shove herself along and topple. She shoved herself along. She toppled. Then she turned around and looked at the assembled committee ⁓ girl, dog, three women, one hen, eleven cats at various windows ⁓ and there was nothing wrong with her at all, and the ground was simply the wrong thing to be standing on.

"Nothing wrong with her," said Remedios.

"It's the ground that's wrong," said Angustias. She licked a finger and held it up, and read the air off it the way she read a plank, along the grain. "Flat ground, small wind. She needs the sea cliff and a night nor'easter under her." A pause. "Thursday."

"Thursday," agreed Milagros, from the doorway, in the tone of someone confirming an appointment she had made weeks ago with the weather personally.

"Thursday, then," said Remedios. "At dark. Garachico." And the pardela was carried back inside, where she laughed at the box, the yard, the wind, and Thursday.

Thursday came down the mountain with them.

They went at dusk on the old camino real, the stone mule-road that drops off the shoulder of the mountain toward Garachico in long swinging zigzags, worn smooth-backed by four hundred years of feet and hooves and one wheelbarrow that Doña Fefa had once taken down it in 2029 for reasons the road still remembers. Remedios led. Saga carried the box ⁓ "You found her. You carry her. That's the law" ⁓ and the box was so light in her arms that it felt empty, and it laughed at every switchback, the sound bouncing off the old walls and coming back doubled, so that they descended through the twilight inside a small traveling festival of unhinged joy. Sirius took rear guard. The Viscount came as far as the bend where the channel turns away from the road, and there he stopped, because his title is extremely specific and Garachico has its own arrangements. The last Saga saw of him he was sitting bolt upright on the wall in the last of the light with his tail curled over his feet, putting one paw into the water, and taking it out, and considering, and putting it in again.

Below them Garachico came up out of the evening the way it always does: the little roofs, the church tower, the lava delta spread out into the sea like a dark hand ⁓ the town that drowned in 1706 and rebuilt itself directly on top of its own cooled catastrophe, streets over streets, and holds mass every week on its own grave and thinks nothing of it. There was one light in the church, and they did not stop, and the town let them through the way a town lets through people on animal business at dusk, which is to say completely, with two nods and a door closing softly somewhere out of respect.

Garachico's lava shore after dark was black on black on black: the rock, the water, the sky, three darknesses stitched by whitewater. The wind came onshore out of the northeast exactly as promised, cold and briny and businesslike, and the moment it crossed the box the laughing stopped.

That was the strangest part, Saga thought afterward. The whole week she'd known her, the bird had laughed at everything. And the moment she could taste the salt in the air, silence.

They set the box down on a flat black shelf above the spray line. Remedios did not make a ceremony of it. She opened the box and stepped back and said, quietly, in the register she kept for animals and no one else:

"Doña Pardela. Cuando usted quiera." Madam Pardela. Whenever you're ready.

The bird came out onto the lava. She stood a moment, the wind got in under her. lifting her feathers all over so that she doubled in size. Feathers trembling briefly and flattening, readying themselves. She smelled the whole Atlantic at once ⁓ you could see it arrive in her, the entire ocean, the map of it, the noise of it under everything ⁓ and she ran. Down the black shelf, wings out, ugly for six steps, five, three ⁓ and then the wind took the weight the way a partner takes a weight, and she was off the rock and out over the whitewater, low and fast and suddenly, unarguably, perfect: a thing that had been a dropped glove in an onion field, unfolded into the exact shape of the wind that was carrying it.

She went out over the black water and did not look back, because she had never in her life been wrong about where the sea was.

They stood on the shore, four upright shapes and a dog, and the sea worked, and the wind worked, and then ⁓ far out, past the whitewater, from somewhere already unreachable ⁓ the laugh came back. Once. Trailing. Thrown over her shoulder at the whole grounded world and everything in it, at onions, at boxes, at gravity, at Thursday, and the sound skipped in across the water and went up the dark shore and was gone.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Angustias took the carpenter's pencil out from behind her ear and put the end of it between her teeth, and bit down, gently, the way you do with a thing you have bitten a thousand times, her teeth going into the soft pine of it where they had gone in before. She stood like that a while, looking out across the dark water with the pencil in her mouth.

And then something moved on the back of her neck.

It was only air. A little warm breath of it, out of the middle of all that cold wind off the sea, the way a room changes when a door opens behind you. Angustias went completely still. The hair came up on her neck. She did not turn round, and she did not take the pencil out of her mouth, and for two or three seconds she was not on that shore at all.

Then she shook her head, once, hard, the way you do to put a thought back where it came from. She took the pencil out and put it behind her ear, and picked up the empty box, and folded it flat, and tucked it under her arm.

And then she put her other arm around Saga's shoulders, left it there for the first three switchbacks of the climb, and there was no commentary at all.

The climb back up the camino real is long, steep and dark, and nobody minded. Milagros walked at the back and was sometimes not there and then was. Remedios set the pace of a procession of four. Somewhere above El Tanque the fog arrived, the way it comes to walk you the last stretch, and Sirius ranged ahead through it, and Saga carried the flat box under her arm and her thoughts in no order at all: the laugh going out over the water, the stack in the shed that would stay exactly the height it was, the certificate of yes, the man who used people instead of nails. Saga returned home. The sisters returned home.

Her mother was up, of course. Seedlings, lamp, the marigold sweater.

"Well?" said Linnea.

"She flew," said Saga. "She laughed all the way out."

"Good," said Linnea, and meant it all the way down, and put the kettle on.

Saga sat at the laurel-slab table, in the kitchen shaped like a kidney bean, in the loft-shadow of a man she'd never met, and worked the last of the amber sweet out of its waxed paper, and asked, in the voice of someone asking about the weather:

"What was the winter of the goat?"

Linnea stopped. It was a full stop, kettle in hand, and her face did several things at once, and every one of them was worth the whole walk home.

And up at the finca, at that same hour, three women who had walked to Garachico and back in one night got their boots off at last and put their feet in a basin of hot salt water in front of the fire.

"Ay," said Milagros, with her eyes shut. "Ay. The heat."

"Mm," said Remedios. "Mm. Oh, that is good."

"Hold still," said Angustias, working her thumbs up into the arch of Remedios's foot until Remedios made a noise she does not usually make in front of people. "Stop kicking. Yes. There. I know. I know."

"Don't stop."

"I am not stopping."

"Ay, madre."

"You next," Angustias said to Milagros, "and then somebody can do mine, because I am dying."

And outside on the cobbled path, coming up late from the village with a basket of chestnuts on her hip, the Perdomo woman slowed, and then stopped altogether outside the lit window, in the cold, in the dark, and listened to all of it.

She has been walking past that house for forty years. She has never once actually seen anything. She went on up the path with her chestnuts and her certainty, and the sisters soaked their feet, and the fire went down, and out over the black water somewhere west of the island a bird who had never flown before was flying.

A few words for the curious

  • Pardela: the Cory's shearwater ⁓ pardela cenicienta ⁓ the big gray seabird of the Canary night. Nests in the cliffs, crosses whole oceans, comes ashore only to breed, and cannot take off from flat ground: she needs wind and a drop, like certain other magnificent personalities. The call is famous ⁓ a wild wheezing laugh in the dark that has been frightening and delighting these islands for as long as there have been ears.
  • La campaña: real, and it ran on this island for more than twenty-five years ⁓ the Campaña de Rescate de Juveniles de Pardela Cenicienta, every autumn, roughly mid-October to the start of December. The young birds fledged toward the light off the water, the towns were brighter than the water, and they came down. So the island picked them up: patrols, volunteers, schoolchildren, a number you called, a recovery center up at La Laguna, and a release back over the sea. In a normal year Tenerife alone rescued between two and three and a half thousand birds and got better than nine out of ten of them back to the water. The worst place for it was never the dark north; it was the bright south ⁓ Adeje, Arona, the hotel coast ⁓ where they fell out of the sky by the hundred, every single October, into the car parks. It was one of the kindest things the old world did on a fixed annual schedule, and it existed entirely to undo something the old world was also doing on purpose.
  • El camino real: the old royal road ⁓ the stone mule-track that drops from the shoulder of the mountain down to Garachico in long switchbacks. Four hundred years of feet. It does not need maintaining; it needs respecting, which is different, and cheaper.
  • Garachico: the town the volcano drowned in 1706, which rebuilt directly on top of its own cooled ruin and holds mass on its own grave without thinking twice. If you have to give a sea thing back to the sea, you could do worse than a town that knows the transaction personally.
  • The loaf-hold: both hands down over the wings, gathered kindly, thumbs where you can count them. The beak is a stapler. Offer the respectful diagonal, and different thumb next time.
  • El Vizconde de la Acequia Alta: the Viscount of the Upper Water Channel. An enormous title for a stone gutter eleven inches wide, granted by Remedios on the grounds that he has spent his entire life sitting at one bend of it trying to stop the water with one paw, which is a devotion, and devotions ought to come with land. He is black, limps on the left, is paid in sardine heads, does not leave his jurisdiction, and has never once in his life been wrong about where Saga was. He is not picked up. He is not called. He arrives, and is received, and precedes you through doors. He has never caught the water. He has never stopped trying.
