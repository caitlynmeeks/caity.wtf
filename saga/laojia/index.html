Here is what she does, and it took her about four years to be able to say it out loud without the sentence turning into an apology.

She keeps a node. She keeps the whole Tchintián end of Príngales: eleven rooftops in the quarter, the hop up to the ridge, the store-and-forward that holds the north's mail when the ridge link is down, and the two ancient boxes in the back room that do the holding. Before that she trained at a university in Massachusetts that no longer functions in the way it did, and then went to Hangzhou and spent six years building systems of a scale that is now genuinely difficult to describe to anybody, including herself, and then came here for two weeks in the autumn of 2028 to see her grandmother.

She has been here fourteen years.

That is the whole of it and she does not tell it that way, because told that way it is a tragedy, and she has found over fourteen years that people who are told a tragedy will do a face at you, and then you have to manage the face.

So when people ask, she says: I came back for a visit and then it got complicated.

❦

The quarter woke up at five, the way it does, with the big tiendas.

You have to understand what a tienda was here, before, to understand what one is now. Two generations of families out of Qingtian ran shops on this island: the bazares, the everything-shops, four thousand items under one roof at a price nobody could explain, open at hours no Spanish shop had ever been open, stocked entirely from containers that came in through Santa Cruz on a schedule as reliable as tide.

That was the arrangement. That was the whole arrangement, for forty years, and it worked, and it made a quarter.

And then the containers stopped.

Priscilla was here for that. She watched it happen from her grandmother's kitchen: the shelves going down in stages, front to back, the way a tide goes out. First the electronics. Then the plastics. Then the toys, which took longest, because nobody was buying toys. By 2031 the biggest tienda on the street had four aisles of nothing in it and one aisle of rice.

Nobody in her family has ever once called that a disaster in her hearing. What her uncle said, in 2031, in the back of the shop, with his hands flat on the counter, was: "Bueno. ¿Y qué sembramos?"

Fine. So what do we plant?

❦

They plant wheat.

They plant wheat and barley and a little spelt on ninety hectares of terraced valley floor above La Orotava that four families bought together in 2032 for an amount of money that was already becoming a joke, from a man who was leaving and who thought he had done extremely well out of them.

And now, eleven years later, if you eat bread anywhere on the north of this island, it started in that valley.

Priscilla did not do this. She wants that recorded properly, because she has heard the version where she did, and the version where she did is a nicer story and it is not true. She was thirty and a foreigner in her own family's business and she knew about distributed systems.

The people who did it were her uncle, who had run a bazar for twenty-two years; her mother, who had run the accounts of that bazar and turned out to be the person who understood water rights; two men from the Perdomo family who came up and taught them what would grow, out of an old rivalry with somebody else entirely; and her grandmother, who was seventy-six, and who had grown wheat as a girl in Zhejiang, and who walked out onto the first terrace in 2032 and put her hand in the soil and said one word in a dialect nobody younger than sixty in that valley can speak, and then told them, in Spanish, that it would do.

❦

The sowing takes eight days and everybody comes.

That is not sentiment, it is arithmetic: ninety hectares, no working tractor since 2036, two mules, and about forty people who can hold a seed bag. The AI engineer comes. The AI engineer has come every November for ten years, and she is good at it now, which took a while and about which nobody was gentle.

She went up on the Tuesday in the back of the flour truck with six others and a dog.

The valley above La Orotava on a November morning is one of the reasons people used to come to this island and pay money. The cloud sits up on the ridge and does not come down. The terraces run in long soft steps all the way down the valley with the stone walls holding them, walls somebody built by hand a hundred and fifty years ago for bananas and vines and then for nothing at all for thirty years, and now for this. The soil is black. The air smells like turned earth and eucalyptus and, faintly, all day, of the bakery smoke coming up from the town.

Her grandmother is eighty-seven now and does not sow. She sits at the top of the first terrace on a folding chair with a blanket and a thermos and watches, and criticizes, and is correct.

They worked the top terraces in the morning and the light was flat and good.

Priscilla walked her rows with the bag on her hip and her hand going in and out in the rhythm you get after about two seasons and never lose, and her mother worked the row beside her and talked for four hours without requiring an answer, which is a thing her mother has always done and which Priscilla has come to understand is not conversation but company.

At the turn of the ninth row her mother said, without any change of tone:

"Xiaowen, your hands are cold."

Nobody else on this island calls her that. There are perhaps nine people alive who do. Her name at home is not the name on the mesh and not the name Anna uses and not the name she used for six years in Hangzhou, and there is no version of the sentence what is your actual name that she has ever been able to answer in a way that satisfies the person asking.

"They are fine, Mamá."

"They are cold. You sit in that room."

"I sit in that room."

"Mm," said her mother, and went on down the row, and forty minutes later there was a cup of something hot standing on the wall at the end of Priscilla's row, and neither of them said anything about it at all.

At the break they ate on the wall in the sun.

There was bread from their own wheat, which is a sentence that took eleven years to become possible and which nobody in that valley has ever got tired of; and there was papas arrugadas and a green mojo; and there was, because her aunt is a serious person, a pot of chili oil that has been made in this family for four generations and that has migrated, over the last decade, into the cooking of about a third of the north without anybody quite noticing it happen.

Two of the Perdomo men ate with them, and the older one put chili oil on his papas with the air of a man doing something faintly illicit, and Priscilla watched her aunt watch him do it and say nothing and be extremely pleased.

She thought, not for the first time: nobody is ever going to write this down.

There is no record of any of it. There is no census. There is no ledger anywhere that says a bazar family from Qingtian and a cheese family from Vilaflor spent eleven years teaching each other how to feed a coastline, and that it started because somebody had an argument with somebody else in 2032, and that the chili oil went north.

She keeps a network for a living. She knows exactly what an unrecorded thing is worth and exactly how fast it goes.

Her grandmother's village is called Qingtian, and the quarter Priscilla lives in is named after it, in Canarian mouths, worn down over forty years into Tchintián ⁓ which is what the whole island calls it now, including everybody who lives there, including her.

She has never seen it.

She has been to Hangzhou, which is a hundred and fifty kilometers from it and might as well be Boston. She had six years to make that trip and there was always a reason. She was going to go in the spring of 2029 and take her grandmother.

There is a word for the place your family is from ⁓ the ancestral house, the home village, the one you belong to whether or not you have ever set foot in it ⁓ and it is lǎojiā, and it is used constantly and casually by people who have never been, and Priscilla has said it her whole life about a valley in Zhejiang she could not pick out of a photograph.

The joke, which she has never once said out loud to anybody because there is nobody to say it to, is that she is now permanently resident, for the rest of her life, in a place she is from.

She got here for a fortnight in 2028 and the door shut, and she is not stranded far from home. She is stranded at it. That is a different thing and it is a smaller thing and she is well aware that it is a smaller thing, and it turns out that the size of a thing has very little to do with how long you lie awake in it.

A few words for the curious

  • Lǎojiā [LAO-jyah]: the ancestral home. The village or county your family comes from, which remains yours whether or not you have ever been there. It is not a sentimental word and it is not usually said with any weight; it is used the way you would use back home, constantly, by people three generations and nine thousand kilometers removed from the place in question.
  • Tchintián: the Chinese-Canarian quarter, between Santa Cruz and La Orotava. The name is Qingtian ⁓ a county in Zhejiang province ⁓ come down through forty years of Canarian mouths. The overwhelming majority of the Chinese families in Spain trace back to Qingtian and to Wenzhou next door, in a chain of emigration that has been running since before the First World War. Almost nobody in Tchintián has ever seen it.
  • The tiendas: the everything-shops. Four thousand items under one roof, open at hours no other shop kept, stocked entirely by container. It was a whole economy and it worked for forty years and it ended in about eighteen months. What the families did next is the part nobody outside the quarter knows: ninety hectares of terraces above La Orotava, bought together in 2032, and now the bread of the north.
  • Las tres Marías: what the Canaries call the three stars of Orion's belt. Nobody else in Spain calls them that. There are, in one family in Tchintián, four names for those three stars, and which one gets used depends entirely on who is standing next to you.
  • Chili oil: made in one family for four generations, and now, without any single moment you could point at, in about a third of the kitchens in the north. There is no record of this anywhere. There is no record of most of it anywhere.
