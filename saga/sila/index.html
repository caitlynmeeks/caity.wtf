Sila

El Fraile, and then Querencia de los Helechos. Three days in January, 2042.

Welcome back to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Caitlyn Meeks. Thanks for being here.

The mesh unit in the yard at El Fraile lived in a bread tin.

It was not a joke and it was not improvised. The tin was steel, the tin had a hinge, the tin sat under the awning where the drip came off the corner, and in fourteen years the thing inside it had never once got wet. Pipaluk put it there. She had strong feelings about the difference between a solution and a habit, and she would tell you, at length, that a habit is a solution somebody stopped looking at.

She checked it at six in the morning because she was awake, and there was a message from Anna, sent at some hour that was nobody's business.

are you still working in january or do you go dormant like a proper artist

Pipaluk typed with two fingers and no urgency.

i am working. i am always working. the ground does not care what month it is.

good. come up.

come up where.

here. bring skjørn. bring nothing else, we have everything.

that is what people say when they do not have everything

we have everything, typed Anna, and also i have a girl living in my house now and i have not told you about her and i am not going to do it on a mesh unit at four in the morning.

Pipaluk read that twice.

She and Anna have known each other for twenty-one years. They met in an office in Roslindale in a city that is not there anymore in the way it was, and the first thing Pipaluk ever said to Anna Doherty was a complaint about a doorway. Twenty-one years. There is a shorthand between them by now that could fit on a stamp.

how long can we stay, she typed.

there is always a bed in the long house. always. that is the whole point of the long house.

that is not an answer.

it is the only answer we have, typed Anna. nobody here has ever counted the days anybody stayed. come on friday. stef is going up empty.

Pipaluk closed the bread tin, and latched it, and stood in the yard in the cold with her hands in her sleeves, looking at the sky over El Fraile going from black to the color of a dirty window.

Then she went in and woke her husband and told him they were going north, and Skjørn ⁓ who has been married to her for twenty-seven years and has learned exactly one thing about it ⁓ said "how long," and then, before she could answer, "I will bring the wool."

❦

Skjørn Thorbjornsson was Faroese, which on this island meant he was the only one, a fact he raised roughly weekly and had never once got tired of.

He was fifty-four and built like something you would tie a boat to, and he was the most talkative man in the south of the island. He had three anecdotes about every human being he had ever met, including people he met once, in an airport, in 2009. He asked questions and then actually listened to the answers, which is rarer than charm and is mostly what people mean when they say charm. He was delighted by things. He was delighted by nearly everything. Fourteen years ago the world had ended, and he had spent every day since finding some part of the wreck genuinely fascinating, and it was not a performance and it was not denial. Pipaluk married it on purpose in 2015 and had never once regretted it, and said so about as often as he mentioned being Faroese.

He also knitted.

He had learned it from his grandmother in Vágur before he could read, and he did the old patterns from memory, the ones with names, and he did them fast, and he did them while talking, which meant he did them almost constantly. There were perhaps forty people on Tenerife walking around in jerseys with the weather of a rock in the North Atlantic worked into the shoulders and no idea that they were.

He got in the truck with a bag of wool and started a story before the doors were shut. Pipaluk got in with a folding rule, two chisels, a notebook, and no clothes to speak of.

"You have brought your tools," Skjørn said, mid-sentence, without dropping the story, "and no trousers."

"I have trousers."

"You are wearing trousers. Those are the trousers. That is the entire allocation." He cast on. "Anyway ⁓ so the man says to me, and remember he has never once been on a boat ⁓ "

Stef drove them up the mother road and then off it, and the island did the thing it does, which is stop being one place and start being another without ever telling you when.

Pipaluk had made that drive maybe six times in fourteen years and it did the same thing to her every time. The south is her home now in the way that a coat you did not choose is your coat: dry, bright, honest, the color of a lion, everything visible at all times. And then you come up over the shoulder of the mountain and down the other side and the world puts a hand over your eyes.

The fog took them somewhere above El Tanque. Not weather, exactly. A place, that you drive into.

"Sila," she said.

Skjørn made a noise of agreement without looking up from his knitting.

There is no good way to put sila into Spanish or English, and Pipaluk gave up trying about thirty years ago. It is the weather. It is also the air, and the outside, and the whole world considered as a single thing that is going on around you whether you attend to it or not, and it is also, in a way that people from temperate countries find annoying, something like consciousness. Not yours. Its.

You do not have weather in Greenlandic. Weather has you.

The laurisilva closed over the road, and the fog came through it in slow ropes, and every leaf on both sides was carrying water and letting it go, and the sound through the open window was not rain and was not silence. Stef changed down and did not say anything, because Stef never says anything, which is one of the four best things about Stef.

And then the trees opened and there was Querencia.

❦

Here is what Pipaluk saw, and she is professionally obliged to look at things properly, so it took her about forty seconds and she has been thinking about it since.

It was bigger than she expected and smaller than it sounds. Three hundred people, she had been told, which on this island makes it a town and not a village, but it did not read as either, because it was not laid out. It had grown in rings. There was a two-level terraced square at the bottom of it with a fountain, and everything else had accumulated around that the way lichen accumulates: houses that had been animal pens, houses that had been campers and then were not, a long stone hall along one side with a chimney going, and then, going up the slope into the trees, more of it, thinner and thinner, until you could not say where the town stopped and the forest started, because nobody had ever decided.

There were no straight lines anywhere.

Not one. The walls were curved and plastered, and the plaster was set with broken tile in patterns that were clearly the work of about nine different people over about fifteen years, some of them children. Doorways were not rectangles. Benches came out of walls as though the wall had thought of it. There was a bread oven that was also a bench and that was also, from the north, a very large sleeping animal.

Pipaluk got out of the truck in the fog and stood there with her bag over her shoulder and did not move for long enough that Skjørn came and stood beside her.

"Who designed this?" she said.

"Nobody," said Stef, hauling her chisels out of the bed. "It just happened."

And Pipaluk, who has spent her whole working life looking at what people build and saying the accurate thing about it out loud whether or not anybody wants to hear it, did not say anything at all.

Anna came across the square at a speed that was not dignified.

She had flour to the elbow and she was wearing the koi-print square knotted at her throat, and she got hold of Pipaluk by both arms and looked up at her ⁓ Pipaluk is a great deal taller ⁓ and said "you look terrible," and Pipaluk said "you look old," and then neither of them said anything for a while, in the middle of a square, in the fog, in front of everybody, and a woman going past with a basket did not comment.

And then Skjørn got to her, and picked her up.

Straight off the ground, both arms, the way you pick up a child or a sack, while she made a noise of complete outrage into his shoulder, and he was already talking.

"Anna Doherty. Anna Doherty. You are a disgrace, you live in a cloud, nobody can get to you, I had to be driven, and look at you, you are covered in flour, are you happy? You look happy. Are you happy? Don't answer, you will lie, I will ask the girl."

"Put me down."

"In a moment." He put her down. "Thirty years."

"Thirty years," Anna agreed.

Here is the part that has to be said, because the whole visit rests on it and nobody at Querencia knew: Anna Doherty and Skjørn Thorbjornsson started a company together in a Reykjavik apartment in 2012. Two rooms, four people, and a plan to take the entire games industry apart from underneath. It worked. That was the trouble with it. It scaled and it took money and it turned by degrees into precisely the thing it was built to destroy, and neither of them has ever completely got over having been good at it.

The first time Anna ever saw him he was face-down on the couch of that apartment at eleven in the morning with one shoe still on.

She has told that story perhaps six hundred times. He has never once asked her to stop.

Then he stood back and produced from his bag a jersey in a deep undyed brown-gray, the pattern worked in bands across the yoke, and put it into her hands and did not make anything of it.

"It is January," he said.

"Skjørn ⁓ "

"It is January," he said, as though that settled it, and it did.

The long house has a bed in it for anybody, and this is not hospitality. It is policy.

Pipaluk had this explained to her three separate times by three separate people in her first hour and each of them explained it slightly differently, which she has since decided is the most Querencia fact about Querencia. The version she liked best came from a woman named Idaira, who does the dawn bake at the panadería and had come over to see the visitors on her way home to sleep, and who said it while eating somebody else's bread:

"If there is no bed, people ask before they come. And if people ask before they come, they only come when it is important." She shrugged. "Then you never see anybody."

The hall itself was one long room under old pine beams, with a slab table down the middle that would seat forty and a kitchen at the end built to feed a village. It was raised in the coliving years, when it held yoga classes and ecstatic dance, and Pipaluk was told this twice as well, both times with the same expression, which was the expression of people who have inherited a building from an earlier and sillier version of themselves and have decided to be fond about it.

Now it holds the communal dinners, and the weddings, and the wakes, and the meetings, and everything that has to happen indoors when it is wet.

Cooking runs on a rotation. Everybody takes a turn. Pipaluk asked who organizes the rotation and got told "it is on the board," and asked who keeps the board, and got a shrug of tremendous warmth and no information whatsoever.

❦

The girl was at dinner.

Pipaluk had been told there was a girl and had not been told anything else, and it took her most of the meal to work out which one, because there were nine people under twenty at that table and they were all shouting.

Then she saw her. Sixteen, maybe. Sitting one place back from the noise with her hands around a cup. Watching the room the way you watch a room when you are still checking whether it is safe to relax in it, and doing it well, and not knowing that anybody could see her doing it.

Pipaluk knows that face from the inside of her own.

She did not go and talk to her, because a woman of fifty-six looming at a girl over dinner is a way of making a girl into a project. She waited two days.

What she did instead was watch a fourteen-year-old named Saga Reyes come down the table with a plate and put it in front of the girl and sit down next to her without asking, and say something, and the girl laughed, and the noise of the room closed over it.

Ah, thought Pipaluk. Good. She has one.

The water stopped on Saturday, at about nine in the morning, in the middle of everybody's day.

It did not stop dramatically. There was no bang. The tap in the long house kitchen coughed twice and went to a dribble and then to nothing, and a man Pipaluk had not met said, without any alarm at all, "ah, we're off," and dried his hands.

Within four minutes it was clear that the entire top half of the settlement was dry.

Within seven minutes, somebody had knocked on the long house door and put their head in and said "line," which was apparently a complete sentence.

And within twenty minutes Pipaluk was walking up out of the square with a canvas bag on her shoulder, because when a stranger who is an earthworks artist is standing in your hall on the day the water goes, you take her with you, and everybody involved understood this without one word being spent on it.

❦

She has thought about that walk more than anything else that happened to her all year.

Querencia gets its water the way this island has always got its water, which is from the fog. Not from rain. From the fog. The laurisilva combs it out of the air ⁓ every leaf on every laurel with water beading on it and running down and dropping, all day and all night, a forest quietly milking a cloud ⁓ and it goes into the ground, and it comes out lower down as springs, and four hundred years ago people started putting stone channels in to catch it and it has never stopped working since.

Pipaluk knew this in principle. She had never walked one.

They went up through the terraces above the square: Ferran, who tends the strays and knew the way; a young woman called Auxi who is twelve and had appointed herself; and the two visitors. Ferran is a compact weathered man who could be forty and could be sixty, and he talks the way a person talks who spends most of his time with animals, which is quietly and to no particular audience.

He named things all the way up. Not the plants. The dogs.

"That is Rigel's lot up there," he said, of a distant crackle in the undergrowth. "Six of them. They go along the top and come down at the water. There is Bellatrix in the barranco, four, and Bellatrix does not like men. There is a young one who is going to have to be called something by the spring and I have not decided."

"You have named every dog on this mountain after a star," said Pipaluk.

"After the ones in Orion, mostly, and then I ran out and went outward." He held a branch for her. "The girl called her dog Sirius when she was six. Nobody told her to. I have never said anything to anybody about that."

He said it with tremendous meaning and did not explain, and Pipaluk decided she liked him a great deal.

The channel is called an atarjea, and it is a beautiful thing, and Pipaluk has been going back over it in her head at night ever since.

It was a stone gutter, hand-cut, about as wide as a forearm, running the contour of the hill under the laurels with a fall on it too slight to see and impossible not to hear. Some of it was four hundred years old. Some of it had been rebuilt eight years ago. She could not always tell which was which, and where she could, it was because the new work was better, which she had not expected and which she wrote down.

It was furred green along the sides and clear down the middle. It went under root systems and out the other side. Twice it crossed a gap on a little bridge of laid stone that somebody had built for water to walk over, and nothing about either bridge was decorated, and both of them were lovely.

And every forty meters or so there was a toma ⁓ a take-off, a stone notch with a wooden gate in it, where a household or a terrace or the whole square below can be given the water or not given it, by moving one board.

That is the entire technology. A board. A slot. Somebody who knows whose turn it is.

They found the problem two kilometers up: a laurel branch, maybe as thick as a thigh, come down in the night, and about half a hillside of leaf litter come down with it, and the channel packed solid and the water going over the side and away down the slope in a place it had no business being, which had been quietly washing out the path underneath it for six hours.

"That," said Ferran, "is a morning."

It took them three hours and it was completely absorbing and Pipaluk enjoyed it more than she has enjoyed anything since the summer. You cut the branch out in pieces. You clear the channel by hand, with your hands, because a tool takes the moss off the stone and the moss is doing a job. You pack the washed-out path back with what came off it. And you do not, under any circumstances, walk on the downhill lip while it is wet, which Auxi did, and which is why Auxi went home with one boot full of water and enormous dignity.

Skjørn out-lifted everybody there by a factor of about two and did not stop talking for three hours, and by the end of it Ferran ⁓ who is not a talker and does not encourage them ⁓ was laughing so hard at something about a Faroese wedding that he had to sit down on the branch he was supposed to be cutting.

And once, coming back down the wet lip of the channel with a two-meter section of laurel on his shoulder, he met Auxi coming up, and there was no room, and rather than stop the story he simply turned ⁓ up onto the ball of one boot, the branch going round with him like a spar, all of it in one unhurried revolution ⁓ and came off it facing the other way with his weight already going forward and the sentence not even interrupted.

Auxi stopped dead.

"Nine years," said Skjørn, over his shoulder, without being asked. "Tórshavn. Every Tuesday and Thursday until I was seventeen and got too heavy for the lifts." A pause. "I was not too heavy. That is what they said." Another pause, further off now. "I was a little too heavy."

Talvin's finca sat at the top, on the ridge, where the wires were.

They heard it before they saw it. Pipaluk had been told about the antennas and had assumed some kind of mast, and it was not a mast; it was a whole hillside of wire strung between poles and old trees, and when the wind came up the ridge the wires sang ⁓ properly sang, a low sustained note with harmonics in it, the sound of a very large instrument being played very slowly by nobody.

She stood in the yard and listened to it for a full minute with her eyes shut.

"You have got the best sound on this island," she said, when the radio man came out. "Do you know that?"

Talvin Jelfs considered this with the seriousness of a man who has never once been complimented on something he did not build on purpose.

"It's the tension," he said. "They go flat in August."

He gave them all tea. He fed them, without asking whether they wanted feeding, from a pot that was clearly the pot he eats out of every day. He is a tall angular Englishman of about forty-three with the manner of somebody who has thought so much in the last decade that talking has become a slightly foreign procedure, and he is, Pipaluk noticed, deeply and uncomplicatedly delighted to have four people in his yard.

She asked him about the line.

And he stood up, and got out a slate with a hand-drawn map on it that was better than any map she had seen since the world went quiet, and talked for twenty minutes about water.

Where every toma is. Which three of them leak, and by how much. Which stretch silts up in November and which stretch never has. The two places where the channel crosses a fault in the rock and has to be watched after tremors. The exact rate the whole system loses to evaporation and moss on a hot week in August, which he knows, because he measured it, over three summers, for no reason anybody asked him for.

Pipaluk listened to all of it and then said, carefully:

"Who does the maintenance?"

"Everybody," said Talvin. "It's a rotation."

"Yes. And who does the rest of it?"

He looked at her with the face of a man who has been asked a question in a language he did not know he was speaking.

"There isn't a rest of it," he said.

She got the truth out of Ferran on the way down, and she had to ask twice, and Ferran gave it up the way you hand over something you were not aware of holding.

The rotation was real. The rotation worked. About sixty percent of the time the rotation was entirely sufficient, and Querencia was not lying to itself about that and neither was anybody else.

And every single morning, at first light, for nine years, Talvin Jelfs has walked the whole line.

All of it. Five kilometers up and five kilometers back, past every toma, with a stick, before anybody else is properly awake. He does not report it. He is not on the board. He has never once said that he does it, and when the branch came down on Saturday he had already been past that point at half past six and had seen nothing, because it came down at eight, and Pipaluk works this out standing on a wet path and has to stop walking for a second.

"Does he know that we know?" she said.

"There is nothing to know," said Ferran. "He walks. He likes walking."

"Ferran."

Ferran looked out at the fog moving through the laurels below them, and did the thing he does, which is answer a question you did not ask.

"He was on his own in a room when it happened," he said. "The whole thing. He watched it go and there was nobody to tell." He started walking again. "A man like that needs to be checking on something. That is all it is. We let him."

They ate at Anna's on the Saturday night, the four of them, which became eleven of them, which became a great deal more than that.

Anna's house stood up the slope from the square on its own fern-pad terrace, and Pipaluk waited outside it for a minute before going in, because she wanted to look at the join.

The bottom half was the same dark volcanic stone as everything above the cultivation line, plastered in places and bare in others. The top half was pallet pine, and it was beautiful ⁓ cut and fitted and weather-treated board by board until the whole upper floor read as one woven object, with a loft window of collected stained glass set in a frame of curved wood, and a long porch facing east so that it took the morning before anybody else got it.

She knew who built it before she asked, because she has been at this a long time and there are only so many people who can do that.

"Croatian," said Anna, in the doorway. "He did the frame on the print too."

"Where is he?"

"Nobody knows," said Anna. "That is a whole evening. Come in, you are letting the heat out."

Inside, it smelled of woodsmoke and sugar and cedar. There was a noren over the back passage that did a small parting motion around her shoulders, and a print of a white wolf-mother in a laurel forest almost identical to the laurel forest outside the window, and up on a corner shelf two framed photographs and a saucer of candles. Pipaluk did not look at the second photograph for very long, because she had been in that room before and she knew what was in it.

The girl was laying the table. She did it without being asked, and she did it wrong, and nobody corrected her, and Pipaluk watched Anna watch her do it wrong.

Skjørn had brought two bottles of something and would not say what.

He got them out on the table with enormous ceremony, and it turned out to be Bier-Bel, the banana beer from the south, which is not rare and is not special and which everybody in that room has drunk a hundred times.

"It is ceremonial," he said, to the general disappointment. "It is not about the beer. Sit down."

They sat down, and there was papas and a great blackened pan of onions and a rabbit that had been in the oven since three, and half a wheel of goat cheese from a quesería two valleys over, and Margarethe's bread, which is the best bread on this island and which Idaira had baked that morning and which everybody at that table knew both of those facts about.

And then Skjørn poured four fingers of banana beer into five cups and stood up.

"Twenty-nine years ago this month," he said, "we shipped."

Anna put her face in her hands.

"We shipped," said Skjørn, to the table at large, "and it did not crash, and there were eleven thousand of them by the Monday, and Anna Doherty was sick in a bin."

"That is not what happened."

"That is exactly what happened. There is a photograph."

"There is no photograph."

"There is a photograph and Gertrude has it," said Skjørn, "and when this island rebuilds photography I am going to be the first customer."

Here is the history, and Ikea got the whole of it that night with her chin on her hand, because Skjørn tells it the way other people breathe.

There were four of them in an apartment in Reykjavik in 2012. Two rooms. One of the rooms was a bedroom until it stopped being one. They had three million from a fund in California whose partners never once visited, and they had a plan, and the plan was ridiculous and specific: the tools that people used to make games were owned by four companies, and those four companies took a cut of everything anybody made forever, and Anna and Skjørn were going to give the tools away and break all of it.

"And we did," said Skjørn, with his hands out. "That is the part nobody believes. We actually did it. For about four years a person in a town with nothing could make a thing and put it out and not ask anybody's permission, and that was us, and it was the best thing I have ever been in a room for."

"And then?" said Ikea.

"And then it worked," said Anna.

She said it flatly, into her cup, and Skjørn stopped talking for a second, which is rare enough that Pipaluk noticed.

Because that is the whole of it, and there is no version of the story where it comes out differently. It worked, and working meant growing, and growing meant money, and money meant the fund, and the fund meant a board, and the board meant a man in a good jacket explaining, patiently, in 2019, that the tools were now a platform, and that a platform has to have a revenue model, and by 2024 the thing that was built to take the toll booths off the road was a toll booth, and the two people who built it were on opposite sides of an ocean not talking about it.

"We ate the ad-tech company," said Skjørn. "I signed it. Nobody made me."

"You were outvoted on everything else that year."

"I signed it," said Skjørn. He raised his cup, entirely cheerfully. "To being outvoted on everything except the worst thing."

They drank. It was, Pipaluk noted, a toast everyone at that table had heard before and nobody had ever ruined.

Anna left in 2016 and went to Boston and made small things for nine years.

That is where Pipaluk came in, in 2021, into an office in Roslindale with a doorway that had been designed by somebody in a hurry, and her opening remark about it has followed her around for twenty-one years and will presumably be on her stone.

And that is where Alice was.

It was Skjørn who said the name, because it is always Skjørn who says the name.

This is a job. Nobody appointed him to it and he has been doing it for fourteen years. He is the one person in Anna Doherty's life who says Alice out loud at a dinner table in an ordinary voice, in the middle of a sentence, without lowering it and without the little pause beforehand that everybody else does and thinks nobody notices.

"Alice would have hated this room," he said, buttering bread.

Ikea looked up sharply. Anna did not.

"She would have loved the house and hated the room," said Skjørn. "Because there is nowhere in it to put your back to a wall and see the door, and she needed that, she always sat like a gunfighter, in restaurants, at parties, at her own wedding ⁓ "

"She did not."

"She sat at her own wedding with her back to a window and I have thought about it for twenty years."

"There were three people at the wedding," said Anna, "and one of them was her brother, and there was no window."

"There was a window."

"There was no window, Skjørn."

"Then why," said Skjørn, delighted, "do I remember the light?"

And Anna laughed.

Then Skjørn told a story about Alice getting thrown out of a conference in 2014 for arguing with a keynote speaker in the corridor, which he told badly and at enormous length and got several details wrong, and Anna corrected him on all of them, which was the point of telling it badly.

And then it went quiet.

And Skjørn ⁓ and this is the whole of why he is good at this ⁓ did not rescue it. He let it sit. He buttered another piece of bread and let a table of people be quiet for as long as they needed, which was about nine seconds, and then Ikea said, "What did she look like?" and Anna got up and took the second photograph off the shelf and put it in the girl's hands.

The celebration was not planned by anybody and could not have been prevented by anybody.

What happened was this: at about half past nine somebody arrived at the door with a jar of something, because word had gone round the square that the Faroese one was up, and the Faroese one had last been up in 2039 and is remembered. And then two more people came. And then Ruymán came with the timple, and then the timple was in the house, and once there is a timple in a house on this island there is a mathematical inevitability about the rest of the evening.

They moved the table.

They moved the table out, onto the long east porch that a Croatian carpenter built to catch the morning, in January, in the fog, with the lanterns lit, and forty people ended up on that terrace over the course of two hours and Anna Doherty fed every single one of them, because there is always more than you think in a house that has been feeding a village for twelve years.

Mateo came up from the square with a battery and something to plug it into.

Auxi did handstands. Somebody's grandmother sang two verses of a folía and then refused a third out of pure theater. Idaira, who had been awake since three that morning, danced for forty minutes and then fell asleep sitting up against the doorframe with a cup still in her hand and nobody moved her.

And Skjørn Thorbjornsson, fifty-four years old, in the middle of a stone terrace in a cloud forest, danced with everybody.

Not well. That is the thing. He has nine years of Tórshavn ballet in his legs and he did not use one minute of it. He danced the way an enormous delighted man dances at a wedding: badly, tirelessly, with total commitment and no technique whatsoever, hands in the air, taking whoever was nearest. He danced with Ikea, who did not want to and then did. He danced with Ferran, who has never danced in his life and was given no choice. He got Anna up, and she came up complaining, and stayed up.

Pipaluk sat on the low wall at the edge of the porch with a cup she was not drinking and watched her husband make an entire village happy on a Saturday in January by simply refusing to be tired.

She has an accurate word for what she felt and she is not going to use it.

Somewhere after midnight the fog came all the way up onto the terrace and put itself between the lanterns and everybody's faces, so that the whole party went soft at the edges and slightly gold, and the music kept going inside it, and forty people danced in a cloud on the side of a mountain in a town that nobody designed.

Sila, thought Pipaluk. Fine. All right. I see you.

❦

She talked to the girl on the third day, and it was not a conversation about anything.

Ikea was on the wall by the fountain with her boots off in January, which is a thing you can do at Querencia because the sun comes through when the fog lifts and the stone holds it. Pipaluk sat down at the far end of the same wall, which is a whole language on its own, and got out her notebook and drew for a while, and after about ten minutes the girl said:

"What's it of?"

"The corner over there. Where the two walls meet and neither of them wanted to."

"That's Idaira's dad's. He built it wrong and then he liked it."

"He built it right," said Pipaluk, "and did not know why," and she showed her the page, and the girl looked at it for longer than politeness needs.

Then Ikea said: "Are you from a cold place?"

"Yes."

"Do you go back?"

And Pipaluk, who has answered this question perhaps two hundred times in fourteen years, and who has a short version and a shorter one, looked at the fog coming up over the roofs of a town that grew itself, and said:

"No."

"Okay," said Ikea.

And that was the whole of it. No follow-up, no sympathy, nothing on the face. She simply took the fact, and put it somewhere, and went on sitting on a wall with her boots off. And Pipaluk understood that she was sitting next to somebody who had recently learned that there are facts you cannot do anything about and that the kindest available response to one of them is okay.

They sat there for another twenty minutes without talking.

It is the most restful twenty minutes Pipaluk has had since 2027.

❦

She built the thing on Sunday morning, before they left, and she did not ask anybody's permission, because she has learned that if you ask permission for a small good thing you get a meeting.

At the second toma above the square, where the path crosses the channel and everyone steps over the water four or five times a day, the crossing was a flat slab, and the slab was fine, and it had been fine for years.

Pipaluk took it up. It took her forty minutes and Skjørn's shoulders.

And she relaid it as three stones instead of one, cut and set so that the water goes under all three and comes out the other side changed: the first stone narrows the flow, the second drops it four centimeters onto a lip she chipped rough on purpose, and the third opens it out again.

That is all. It moves no more water and no less. It saves nobody any labor. It is not an improvement in any sense that could be written on a board.

But now, when you step over the channel above the square, on your way to the bread or down to the fountain or up to the terraces with a bucket, the water makes a noise. A small, specific, absolutely deliberate noise, three notes, that it did not make before and that it will make for as long as those three stones stay where she put them, which she has built them to do for about two hundred years.

Nobody saw her do it. Somebody will find it. Somebody found it that same afternoon, in fact, and by the time the truck went down the hill on Sunday there were four people standing at that crossing taking turns walking over it, and a small argument had already started about who had put it there.

Pipaluk got in the truck and did not look back at them, because she was smiling and she did not want to be caught at it.

"You have made them a bell," said Skjørn.

"I have made them a step."

"Mm," said Skjørn, and got out his wool.

A few words for the curious

  • Sila: Greenlandic, and not translatable in one go. It is the weather. It is also the air, and the outdoors, and the whole world going on around you whether or not you are paying attention to it, and it is also something close to consciousness ⁓ not yours, its. In English you have weather. In Kalaallisut the weather rather has you, and it is a big enough idea that it turns up in the words for reason, for climate, and for the world.
  • La atarjea: the stone water channel, hand-cut, running the contour of the hill with a fall on it too slight to see. Four hundred years of them cross the north of this island. They do not catch rain. They catch fog: the laurel forest combs water out of the cloud all day and all night, leaf by leaf, and the channels take what the forest lets go. It is the oldest working machine on Tenerife and it has no moving parts except a board in a slot.
  • La toma: the take-off. A notch in the channel, a wooden board, and somebody who knows whose turn it is. That is the entire irrigation politics of an island, and people have killed each other over it in other centuries, and up here it is a rotation and a chalkboard.
  • Faroese knitting: the patterns have names and are held in the hands rather than written down. There are perhaps forty people on Tenerife walking around in the weather of a rock in the North Atlantic and no idea that they are.
  • The long house bed: kept empty on purpose. If there is no bed, people ask before they come; if people ask before they come, they only come when it is important; and then you never see anybody. This is presented locally as common sense and is in fact a whole philosophy of hospitality, arrived at by a group of people who mostly do not know they arrived at it.
