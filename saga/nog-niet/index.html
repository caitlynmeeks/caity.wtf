Nog Niet

Ten-Bel, the old Belgian colony on the dry south coast. Early December, 2041.

Welcome back to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Caitlyn Meeks. Thanks for being here.

The invitation was laminated.

That was the first thing wrong with it, and nobody could say why it was wrong, only that a laminated thing arriving in December 2041 was like finding a fresh flower in a drawer. Somebody had a laminator. Somebody had kept a laminator working for twelve years, and had kept the pouches for it, and had decided that this was what the pouches were for.

It came up the mountain in the hands of a boy from the coast road who would not come in, and it was read aloud in the long house at Querencia over dinner, by Mateo, standing on a bench, because that is the only correct way to read a laminated document.

"The Congregation of the Vigilant," he read, "extends its warm hand to the travelling players of the north."

"Warm hand," said Tibor, from the end of the table, with his mouth full.

"You are invited to a Gathering of Traders and Neighbors, to be held at the Hall of Vigilance, Ten-Bel, on the second Saturday of December."

"That's a market," said Yara. "That's just a market."

"There will be an evening of music, refreshment, and fellowship."

"That's not a market."

Mateo held up one finger and kept reading, and his voice did something complicated.

"In recognition of the distance travelled, participating traders will receive one hundred and twenty liters of biodiesel."

The long house went entirely quiet.

A hundred and twenty liters is not a quantity. A hundred and twenty liters is a season. It is the difference between a circuit that runs and a circuit that runs and gets to say yes when somebody in Anaga asks whether the puppet show might possibly come up the ridge. Everyone at that table did the arithmetic at the same time, and Saga watched the whole room lean forward about four centimeters.

Because here is the thing about the Vigilant Prophets of Ten-Bel, and it is not a small thing, and everybody on this island lives inside it whether they think about it or not.

They press the fuel.

Not some of it. There are four working presses on Tenerife and the congregation owns three of them, and the fourth is up in Buenavista and belongs to a cooperative with a waiting list. Which means that every truck on the south coast, every generator, every pump, every boat that is not a sailing boat, and the entire travelling life of La Tropa, runs on a liquid that is made by four dozen families who believe the world is going to end shortly and would very much like to talk to you about it.

They have never once refused anybody. That is important. They sell to atheists, they sell to the Perdomos, they sell to Klaus. They are not cruel with it and they do not withhold it, and they would be genuinely wounded if you suggested they might.

They just set the price. And they set it in a room, among themselves, on a Tuesday, and then that is the price, and the island pays it and says thank you.

"A hundred and twenty liters," said Yara, "is them showing us what they've got."

"A hundred and twenty liters," said Mateo, "is also a hundred and twenty liters." He put the invitation down on the table. "So that's the hook, and everybody in this room can see that it's the hook, and let's all say it out loud now and get it over with."

"It's the hook," said Tibor.

"It's a hundred and twenty liters," said Ruymán.

"It's the hook and it's a hundred and twenty liters," said Yara, "and those aren't opposites."

They argued about it for an hour, pleasantly, over stew, in the way that family argues about everything, which is loudly and with food in their mouths and with anybody's opinion admissible including the eleven-year-old's.

Saga had her case ready before the bowls were cleared. She had three points and she had put them in order, and the order was: one, they needed somebody to work the crowd before the show, and that was her job and had been for two years; two, she had never seen the south in December; and three ⁓ and this was the one she had polished ⁓ if the congregation was going to try something, then the useful thing to have in the room was a person nobody thinks is listening, and she was the best one they had.

She got as far as "one."

"You're coming," said Linnea, from the other end of the table, not looking up. "You're working. Eat your dinner."

"I had three points."

"You can do them in the truck."

"Two of them were quite good."

"Then it will be a nice drive," said her mother.

It was Tibor who settled it, in the end, by putting down his spoon.

"I know these people," he said. "I have known them thirty years. They will feed us, they will be kind to us, they will talk at us for two hours, and they will try to save our souls, and then we will go home." He shrugged with his whole upper body. "It is not the first time somebody wanted my soul, and the ones who want it usually feed you first. This is not the worst arrangement in the world."

"And the fuel?"

"The fuel," said Tibor, "is real. They have never once lied about fuel. About the end of the world, constantly. About fuel, never."

They went down on the Saturday, in the Santana, fuel tank in tow, at an hour that was not morning yet.

The drive south is three hours of the island changing its mind about what it wants to be. Querencia's wet green goes to pine, and pine goes to the wet shoulder of the mountain at the Erjos pass, down winding switchbacks until the eucalpytus grove in Santiago del Teide. Through the tunnel, then everything is rock and tabaiba and light on the other side. By nine the fog was a memory. By ten, they'd stopped in Callao Savaje, where Saga had taken off two layers, and were joined by Auxi, who at eleven years old had been permitted to come on the strict understanding that she was working, and who had brought her own bag of tadpoles. They were sitting next to each other on the tailgate, legs out.

Torbe rode in Yara's backpack with his head out of the top like a periscope with a lion cut. He was seventeen now. He slept most of the drive, which he did not use to do, and Yara had lifted him up into the truck bed instead of letting him make the jump, and neither of them had said anything about it.

Ten-Bel comes at you low and white.

It was built in the sixties by Belgians for Belgians ⁓ a whole holiday colony of flat-roofed bungalows in ranks, a communal pool, a clubhouse, all of it laid out on a grid on a dry slope facing the sea. It was beautiful once in an optimistic, master planned geometric kind of way. It had been going quietly strange for eighty years.

What Saga saw, coming in off the coast road with her legs hanging out the back, was this: bungalow after bungalow with the shutters shut and the paint gone chalky; and then, in among them, one with a garden so tidy it hurt, and then four in a row with vegetables where the ornamental gravel had been; and everywhere, on the low white walls, faded to almost nothing, the same painted phrase in three languages, repeated at intervals along the whole grid like a heartbeat.

WAAKZAAM. VIGILANTE. WATCHFUL.

"That is new," said Tibor, who had come up to lean on the cab. "Fourteen years. They did that in the spring I lost the ferries."

"They painted the whole town?"

"The whole town. In one week." He shook his head slowly, and he was not laughing. "You have to understand. These people have been announcing the end of the world since before your father was born. 1984. 1991. 2000. 2012, that was a big one, they had shirts. And in all that time, in all those years of being certain ⁓ " he held up a finger " ⁓ they never painted anything. Not one wall. Because you do not paint for a thing that is coming next month."

He looked out at the white grid going past, and the faded words on it, at intervals, like a pulse.

"And then the cables go, and the flights stop, and the shops empty, and everybody down here is frightened. And that is the week they went and got the paint."

"So they thought it was the real one."

"They knew it was the real one. They were completely sure." Tibor spread his hands. "It was the surest they have ever been about anything, and they put it on every wall in this colony where God could see it."

"But it did end," said Saga. "Sort of. Didn't it?"

Tibor laughed. It was not a kind laugh and it was not an unkind one.

"Nog niet," he said. "That is what they say. Not yet. Whatever happens, they look at it, and they check it against the book, and it does not match, so it is not the one. Twelve years the ships stop coming and the lights go out and half the island leaves, and they say" ⁓ and here he did a voice, a flat patient Flemish voice, and it was clearly an imitation of somebody specific ⁓ "'nog niet.'"

Saga thought about that for the rest of the way in.

She had grown up in a world that had already ended before she was born, which meant it had never ended at all, which meant it was simply the world. She had never once thought about it as an ending. And here were four dozen families who had been waiting eighty years for a thing that had already happened all around them, and had not noticed, because it had come in the wrong costume.

She got out her notebook and wrote: they missed it.

The Hall of Vigilance is the ugliest building Saga has ever loved on sight.

It sits at the top of the grid where the colony's clubhouse used to be, and it is a great gray concrete box with narrow vertical windows set deep, like a fort that has decided to be shy about it. Everything else in Ten-Bel is white and low and horizontal. The Hall is the opposite of Ten-Bel, and the opposite of Querencia too, where Saga had grown up in rooms that had no corners at all and doorways shaped like the space a person takes up.

You walk into that lobby and your shoulders come up. It is not a feeling you can argue with.

But Saga was not looking at the concrete. Saga was looking up.

Here is a thing about Saga Reyes that her family finds funny and that she has never once found funny. At a show ⁓ any show, anybody's show ⁓ she does not watch the show. She watches the works. She has spent her whole life backstage in a family that is the stage, and what she loves is the machinery of the trick and not the trick: the tape on the floor, the sandbag on the line, the person crouched in the dark counting. When she was nine she made her father explain the difference between a lamp that floods and a lamp that spots, in the cave, in the middle of a set, and he did, and she has never stopped noticing since. Tibor's cave is fairy lights and seven layers of fluorescent paint. Ruymán's builds are salvage and prayer. The fire is Yara and nothing else.

She had never in her life stood underneath a real one.

Because bolted across that lobby ceiling, running wall to wall, was a proper steel truss ⁓ the kind you hang from, not the kind you lean on ⁓ and off it, in a row, at intervals, were six theater lamps, big ones, with shutters and barn doors and colored gel still in the frames, and cables running back along the truss and away into the dark toward a booth. Aimed at the doors. Aimed so that whoever came through those doors would walk into light.

Somebody had built this place as a theater, sixty years ago.

And somebody was still running it.

"You'd be surprised," said a voice, "how much of it still works."

The girl was standing about a meter away, and had clearly been standing there for a while.

She looked exactly like every other girl Saga had seen since they came through the gate. Except for the tape. And except that while the rest of them had smiled at the visitors with their whole faces, this one was looking at the ceiling with Saga, at the same six lamps, with her head at the same angle.

"Which ones are dead?" said Saga.

The girl's eyes came down and landed on her, and something in her face opened about a millimeter and then shut again.

"Two and five," she said. "And four is on the wrong circuit, so if you kill the house lights, four stays on. Everybody thinks that's a fault." A pause. "It isn't."

"What is it?"

"It's four being on the wrong circuit," said the girl, "which I have never mentioned to anybody, and which nobody has ever asked about in eleven years."

Somebody called from across the lobby ⁓ "Ikea!" ⁓ and she was gone, walking fast, with the flat unhurried stride of somebody who has been walking that floor her entire life.

Saga stood in the lobby of a concrete fort with her mouth slightly open and her whole chest going off like a struck bell.

She got out the notebook and wrote: IKEA. And under it: 4 is on the wrong circuit. And under that, because she was thirteen and could not help herself, she drew a small box and left it unticked.

It did not happen the way you would think. Nobody demanded anything. There was no moment where it turned. It happened because they opened the pool.

Ten-Bel has a pool. It is twenty-five meters long and it is the reason the colony exists, and it is the single most extravagant object Saga had ever stood next to in her life. Querencia has a cistern that becomes a swimming hole for six weeks in August when nobody needs the water. This was a pool. Tiled. Filtered. Warm, because they had run black pipe over the whole clubhouse roof in some year nobody mentioned and the sun down here does what it is told.

They opened it at five, and they opened it for everybody, and the sound that went up out of that market was not a polite sound.

Saga was in the water within four minutes.

She is not going to apologize for this. There were fifty children in that pool and about eighty adults and it was the best hour of the entire year: Auxi doing handstands, the Chacho brothers immediately and inevitably attempting something off the diving board that got them shouted at by a woman with a whistle who had clearly been waiting eleven years to shout at somebody off a diving board, Mateo floating on his back in the middle with his eyes shut and an expression of complete religious peace. Somebody's grandmother did four slow lengths of an immaculate breaststroke and got a round of applause. The congregation's own kids ⁓ who Saga had seen all afternoon standing at the edges with their arms folded ⁓ were in there screaming like anybody.

And in the changing rooms, on benches, with a smiling sister at the door handing them out, there were towels for everybody, and folded white bathrobes, and hooks and baskets for your things.

You see how it works. Nobody asked anybody to undress. You undress to swim. Everybody on this earth undresses to swim. The baskets were for your clothes because that is what baskets in a changing room are for, and the robe was because you cannot walk about wet in December, and the sister at the door had baked some of the cakes.

They came out of that water at half past six, warm and loose and happier than they had been in a year.

The baskets were gone.

Not stolen. Nothing so crude. There was a girl of about fifteen standing exactly where the baskets had been, apologizing to everybody, explaining that the sisters had taken everything down to the wash-house as a kindness, because the traders had been on the road since before dawn in this heat and it was no trouble at all, and everything would come back clean in the morning, and did anybody need another towel.

She was so sorry. She meant it. She had been given a job and she was doing it well.

It was a generous offer. It was, in fact, plainly the correct thing to do at nine o'clock at night on an unlit road in December, and every trader in that hall would have made the same offer at their own house.

He was genuinely, absolutely, completely sincere. Saga disliked him on sight and could not have given one reason for it.

"That's the good age," he said, kindly, and turned to look at her, and his face was completely open, and there was nothing behind his eyes but goodwill. "That's the age where a person starts wanting a use for themselves. Everyone gets it and almost nobody gets to have it. You're going to feel it very soon, if you haven't, and it feels like being hungry in a room with no food in it."

Saga could not have explained, then or now, why that sentence was the most frightening thing anybody had ever said to her. It was true. That was the trouble with it. It was completely true, and he had handed it to her the way you hand a child a sweet, and he was still smiling, and behind them two hundred people in white were nodding along to BOSSA 2.

"We have a use for people here," said Eugen.

"I've got one," said Saga.

"You have a show," he said, gently, and patted the floor twice beside him as if settling a dog, and got up, and went away to be kind to somebody else.

She sat on the floor at the front in a robe with her arms inside it, next to her father, in a concrete hall three hours from home with her own clothes in a wicker basket somewhere behind a door, watching a sweating man rap about the armies of heaven over a bossa nova preset, and she was not frightened, exactly, and she was not laughing either.

She was working.

Because there were six lamps on that bar, and two of them were dead, and one of them was on the wrong circuit, and a girl with tape around her wrist had made absolutely certain that she knew it.

Saga leaned back on her hands, in the dark at the front, and looked up past the lights at the ceiling of the Hall of Vigilance.

There was a catwalk up there. There was a fly system, with ropes tied off at a rail on the stage-left wall. And set into the boards of that stage, about two meters from where Brother Wouter was at that moment explaining, at length, and in rhyme, why the calendar is not a wall, there was the unmistakable seam of a trap door.

Nog niet, Saga thought.

Not yet.

She got out her notebook, under the robe, where nobody could see it, and she did not write a single word. She just turned to a clean page and left it open on her knee in the dark, ready.

A few words for the curious

  • Nog niet: Flemish and Dutch for not yet. The congregation has been announcing the end of the world since the nineteen-eighties, and has been wrong about it in 1984, 1991, 2000, and 2012. When the actual world went quiet ⁓ ships, flights, lights, half the island's people ⁓ they checked it against the book, found that it did not match, and continued waiting.
  • Ten-Bel: a real Belgian holiday colony on the dry south coast, built in the nineteen-sixties, flat-roofed concrete bungalows in ranks on a grid, with a pool and a clubhouse and a great deal of optimism. The optimism has worn off unevenly. WAAKZAAM ⁓ VIGILANTE ⁓ WATCHFUL went onto the low walls in three languages in the spring of 2027, in one week, when the cables were cut and the flights stopped and it was finally, obviously, unmistakably happening.
  • Bier-Bel: banana beer, brewed at Ten-Bel, sour and yeasty and served cold out of ceramic jugs. By a historical accident nobody planned, the congregation ended up holding the island's beer and biodiesel between them, which turned a small strange sect into a small strange sect that everybody has to be polite to.
  • The Sankyo MelodyMate MM-3: a plastic keyboard, sixteen rhythm presets. It has outlived the company that made it, the shop that sold it, the currency that bought it, and the internet that reviewed it. It is going to outlive everybody in this story. Preset four is BOSSA 2.
  • ¡Agüita!: literally little water. The Canarian exclamation for would you look at that. It is the island's whole vocabulary of astonishment in three syllables, it is deployed by grandmothers and by thirteen-year-olds alike, and on this occasion it was aimed at a lighting truss.
  • A rig: the steel truss a theater hangs its lamps from, and everything on it. A person who says rig out loud in a lobby is telling you what kind of person they are. Saga has been that kind of person since she was nine, in a cave, in the middle of somebody's set, asking her father the difference between a lamp that floods and a lamp that spots.
  • A laminator: a machine for sealing a piece of paper inside plastic forever. Somebody in Ten-Bel has kept one running for twelve years. Consider what that means about what they think is coming, and about what they have decided is worth making permanent.
