Episode 17

Helvete

Querencia de los Helechos, the upper terraces. Late November, 2041 ⁓ the year the trees came in.

Welcome to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Caitlyn Meeks. Thanks for being here.

Linnea was on the upper terraces before the light was, which was not discipline. It was appetite.

She stood at the top of the coffee terrace with her hands in the pockets of her overalls and read the hillside the way other people read a letter that has finally arrived. The fog was high and thin and moving east. The air had that dry-edged clarity the north gets for a few days in late November, between one Atlantic system and the next, and the forty trees stood in their rows below her with their branches bent, holding on to somewhere between five and ten kilos of the reddest fact of her life.

Eight years. Seeds a Croatian visitor had carried from Yemen in a film canister, because he had heard she was the kind of person who would know what to do with them. Eleven months of germination trays in the kitchen window. Three busses, then forty. Frost scares, wind scares, the spring the goats got in, the year she doubted the elevation and the year the elevation forgave her. Flowering in April ⁓ the whole terrace gone white and jasmine-smelling for nine days, and half the community walking up just to stand in it ⁓ and then the long green summer of watching coffee cherries decide, one by one, to mean it.

They had decided. The rows were red. Not every cherry, never every cherry, but enough, and the fog was moving east, and the next rain was two days out by the smell of it, which meant it was today.

She went down the rows once, alone, in the gray light, and did the thing she would have denied doing if anyone had seen it: she put one hand flat against the trunk of the oldest tree, the first of the original three, the one that had germinated a full week before its sisters as if it had somewhere to be. She did not say anything to it. She was not a person who spoke to trees.

She stood there a while, not speaking to it.

Then she went down to the square and rang the bell they used for work parties, two strokes and one, which meant: bring baskets. No ⁓ not your baskets. My baskets.

❦

They came up through the fog-wet ferns as the light arrived: the Cebolla sisters in matching aprons, already arguing about something that had happened in 2019; the Romanian family from the papas terraces, all four of them, the youngest carrying the ladder nobody would need because Linnea kept her trees topped at picking height; Mateo with coffee ⁓ the old stockpiled kind, the irony traveling in a thermos ⁓ and Anna down from her mountain with her hair tied in the koi-print square, and Saga, and Sirius, who had been told twice already that this was not an event for dogs and had appealed the ruling successfully, as always.

At the edge of the terrace Linnea had set up her table. On the table were the sacks ⁓ flour-cloth, boiled, sun-dried, sewn by her own hands and kept in a chest that nothing else was kept in ⁓ and a basin of water, and a nail brush.

Everyone washed. Everyone showed her their hands, palms and backs, the way you show a nurse. Nobody laughed at this, or rather, everybody had laughed at it in previous years, once each, and had each received the same look, and the custom had matured into something between hygiene and liturgy. The rule was the rule: nothing touched the trees that had touched another farm's fruit that morning. No borrowed baskets, no visiting sacks, no shortcuts up from the market. The plantation was the only one of its kind on the island, and its solitude was not loneliness. Its solitude was the entire security model.

"Ripe means ripe," Linnea said, to the assembly, by way of good morning. "Deep red. The cherry should want to come. If you are pulling, it did not want to come. Green stays. Turning stays. If in doubt ⁓"

"Ask Saga," said one of the Cebolla sisters, because this too was liturgy.

"Ask Saga," Linnea agreed.

Saga, thirteen and a half and appointed to quality control for the third consecutive harvest of her life if you counted the two practice years when there had been almost nothing to control, adjusted the brim of her sombrera ⁓ the ribboned straw hat Doña Pino had given her, which had become her working hat for occasions of state ⁓ and took up her station at the collection table without a word. The composure of a customs officer. Her mother's child, entirely, and both of them would have denied it.

They picked.

There is a rhythm that settles over hand-harvest by the second hour, and the terrace found it: the small papery rustle of leaves, the tick of cherries into cloth, the Cebolla sisters' argument arriving at its familiar stalemate and converting, as it always did, into song ⁓ something Canarian and older than the argument, one sister high, one low, neither conceding the melody either. The fog burned back. The sun came through in stripes. Sirius patrolled the rows with the gravity of a site inspector and was, at intervals, bribed.

Linnea picked the way she did everything: efficiently, and with a private completeness of attention that from the outside looked like detachment and was its exact opposite. Every cherry through her fingers was eight years long. She did not say so. The trees knew, or did not, and either way the work was the same.

At the collection table, Saga sorted. Deep red to the left, doubtfuls back for re-inspection, and one memorable rejection held up between finger and thumb for its picker ⁓ Mateo ⁓ to observe in full daylight.

"It's red," Mateo said.

"It's turning," Saga said.

"It's red on this side."

"Papá. A cherry is not a mood."

Anna, three trees down, laughed her half-second-early laugh, and the terrace absorbed the ruling, and the doubtful pile grew stricter all afternoon on its own.

❦

The weather broke its appointment at three.

Linnea felt it before she saw it ⁓ the wind going round to the northwest a day and a half early, the light hardening, the smell of rain arriving out of schedule like a guest who had misread the invitation. She straightened up among the trees and looked at the sky over the shoulder of the ridge, and the sky looked back with its cards face down, and she said, conversationally, to the entire Atlantic weather system:

"Helvete."

Deployed like a greeting. It was, among other things, how the terrace learned the forecast.

What followed was ninety minutes that the community would retell all winter. Every hand came off the doubtfuls and onto the ripe. The Romanian family worked as one eight-armed organism. Anna's rows and Mateo's merged. Saga abandoned the table, stationed Auxi's honorary counterpart ⁓ the youngest Romanian, aged seven, immensely proud ⁓ as guard over the full sacks, and threw herself down the last unpicked row like a foreman with a deadline, which she was. The wind came up the terrace in gusts that turned every leaf silver side out, the whole plantation flashing like a signal, and the first fat drops were actually falling, actually audible on the big fern fronds below, when the last ripe cherry of the year's first harvest went into the last boiled sack ⁓

⁓ and the sack, overfull, propped in haste against a terrace stone, tipped.

It went over the edge in a long red spill, cherries hopping and scattering down into the fern terrace below, and the whole hillside went silent for the length of one indrawn breath, and everyone looked at Linnea.

Linnea looked at the ferns.

"Well," she said. "Now it is a treasure hunt."

And the whole crew went over the terrace lip into the wet green after them, laughing, rain arriving in earnest now, Sirius plunging ecstatic through the fronds, the Cebolla sisters singing louder against the weather, and they picked the year's coffee for the second time that day, cherry by cherry, out of the ferns of Querencia de los Helechos ⁓ which, as more than one person shouted from inside a fern at the time, was after all what the place was named for.

They lost perhaps two hundred grams to the hillside. Linnea, wringing out her marigold sweater under the eaves afterward, ruled it an acceptable tithe.

❦

That evening the long house did what the long house does when a terrace full of soaked people comes down the hill: it absorbed them.

The community had built it in the coliving years ⁓ Canarian stone, one long hall under old pine beams, a slab table you could land a small aircraft on, a kitchen at the end that could feed a village because it had been designed to. Before the quiet it had held yoga classes and ecstatic dance; since, it had held weddings, meetings, wakes, wet-weather work, and dinner, which is to say it had never once changed its purpose, only its music. It was the radio man's week to cook ⁓ the rotation is the rotation, harvest or no harvest ⁓ and he had been at the big stove since noon while the rest of them picked: the pot of papas and whatever the week had voted into the stew, bread timed to come out when the bell brought everyone down. The table filled end to end, pickers and children and the two households who hadn't picked but arrived fully briefed anyway, steam rising off wet wool, the mojo passed hand to hand without anyone asking. Sirius worked the underside of the table like a professional. The Cebolla sisters, hoarse from singing against the rain, argued in whispers as a courtesy to the room. And the radio man, who lived in the community and kept his antennas at the old finca up the mountain, watched forty wet people eat what he had made, and said almost nothing, and looked, as he generally did at that table, like a man who could not quite believe his luck. Nobody eats alone at Querencia on a harvest night. Nobody, it should be said, eats alone at Querencia much at all.

Afterward they cleared the slab table, and with the rain hammering the long house roof they pulped the cherries by hand ⁓ thumb and forefinger, the fruit giving up its two slick seeds with a sound like tiny punctuation ⁓ and the smell that rose off the fermentation tubs was nothing like coffee and everything like the beginning of it: green, honeyed, faintly of apples, the smell of a promise at its rawest stage. People stayed long past the work's end. Mateo played the recording he had made of the rain arriving over the picking, with the Cebolla sisters' singing buried in it, and nobody asked him what it was for. It was for this.

Two weeks on the drying racks, turned by hand, guarded from the fog under glass Linnea had salvaged for exactly this purpose four years before she needed it. Then the sorting, then the weighing, on the kitchen scale, with the entire household watching the needle.

Seven kilos, two hundred grams of green coffee.

Linnea wrote the number in her planting book, in the column she had ruled for it eight years ago, and closed the book, and made everyone go home.

❦

The tin rode the Guagua to Santa Cruz on a Tuesday in December, wedged between the mail crate and a cage of hens, with Linnea and Saga riding beside it because some freight you accompany.

Margarethe's panadería smelled of Idaira's second bake ⁓ the dawn loaves long gone, the Orotava flour doing its slow brown work in the oven. The espresso machine ⁓ the island's last, kept alive the way other people keep faith ⁓ stood gleaming at the counter's end, and Margarethe took the tin without a word, weighed a portion, and roasted it in her oven in a perforated drum she had built the year the supply ships stopped, cranking it by hand at the oven's mouth, watching the beans through their first crack the way you watch a child's first anything. The smell went through the panadería and out the door and down the street, and people came to the doorway. People stood.

She ground. She dosed. She pulled.

What came out was small and dark and crowned with crema the color of the calima sun, and Margarethe ⁓ who had served coffee to two generations of two countries, who did not perform, whose roast in the before had gone out by courier as far as a certain developer's villa on the island's far side, order standing, gratitude never included ⁓ set the cup in front of Linnea and stepped back from the machine, and folded her hands, and waited with everyone else.

Linnea looked at the cup a moment. Eight years, forty trees, one island, no sea route to anywhere.

She drank.

The panadería held still. Saga watched her mother's face do a thing it did perhaps twice a year, which was to stop administering itself.

"Helvete," Linnea said, very quietly.

Which, from her, translated exactly.

❦

The year of Sunday coffee began that week: a cup for the household after breakfast, a cup for Anna up the mountain, a standing tin behind Margarethe's counter for the machine, and the community kitchen's share, brewed in the big pot on the first Sunday and drunk by forty people in the fountain square, most of whom had picked it, some of whom had picked it twice.

And in the kitchen window at Querencia, where the germination trays had once stood, there was a film canister ⁓ the same one, kept ⁓ and in it Linnea had already counted out the seeds for the next forty trees.

A few words for the curious

Swedish and Spanish this time, and the smell of first crack.

  • Helvete: Swedish. Literally hell; functionally, from Linnea, anything from good morning to the weather has betrayed us to ⁓ once, in a panadería in Santa Cruz, very quietly ⁓ perfection. Context is everything. Her family has learned to read it like weather.
  • Peaberry: most coffee cherries hold two flat-sided seeds; sometimes a cherry makes only one, small and round, and roasts sweeter for it. The Spanish is caracolillo ⁓ "little snail." Linnea's whole plantation descends from peaberry stock out of Yemen, via one Croatian visitor and a film canister.
  • First crack: partway through roasting, coffee beans audibly crack like slow popcorn ⁓ the sound of the sugars committing. Margarethe listens to it the way the radio man listens to the static, which is to say: completely.
  • Sunday coffee: ~1,400 cups a year, if the fog cooperates. It will not be traded far, it will not make anyone rich, and it is, by unanimous informal ruling, the most important agricultural product on the island.
