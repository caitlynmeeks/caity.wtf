Episode 29

Sturmvogel

The villa at Los Menores, above the Adeje coast. A night in October, 2042.

Welcome back to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Caitlyn Meeks. Thanks for being here.

The lights on Klaus Reiter's terrace have been on every night for twenty-two years.

There were twenty-eight of them originally, sunk into the deck and the retaining wall and along the line of the drop, and the Belgian architect had specified them himself in a document that ran to nine pages on the subject of illumination alone. Nineteen of them still work. Klaus has moved the panels twice in thirteen years to keep them fed, which he does not consider an indulgence and would explain to you, at length, as a matter of security and of the value of the asset, and he has never once been asked.

He was on the terrace at half past nine with the second coffee of the evening when the sky dropped something onto it.

He did not see it come. He heard it: a wet slapping thump about six meters to his left, on the pale stone, with a scrape after it.

He stood up so fast that he put his hand in the coffee.

❦

The thing on his terrace was the size of a cat and it was not a cat.

It had come down badly and it was on its front with one wing out, and while he watched it hauled itself upright, dragged the wing back in, and shoved itself forward about a foot on feet that did not appear to be designed for the operation. It stopped. It turned its head and looked at him with an eye like a wet bead.

It was gray-brown on top and white underneath and its beak was pale and hooked with something laid along the top of it, and it smelled, from four meters away, of the sea.

Klaus stood in the light with coffee on his hand and did not move for a long time.

He had lived on this coast for twenty-five years. He had built on it, drained under it, sued over it, and looked at it every morning of his life through a telescope good enough to find the rings of Saturn.

He had never seen one of these before.

❦

His first instinct was the correct instinct of his entire adult life, which is that this was somebody's job and the somebody was not him.

He went inside and told Sonja.

Sonja was at the kitchen table with a lamp and the mending, and she listened, and then she came out and stood in the doorway and looked at it for a while with her arms folded.

"It came out of the sky?"

"It fell. It fell on my terrace."

"Mm," said Sonja.

"It is injured."

"Perhaps." She did not go closer. "Perhaps it is only down."

He looked at her.

"That happens," she said, and went back in.

❦

Here is what Klaus did over the following forty minutes, and it should be recorded properly, because it is the best thing he does in this story and possibly in his life.

He tried to shoo it off the terrace.

It did not go. It shoved itself under the outdoor table and sat there.

He got a broom, and stood over it with the broom for perhaps fifteen seconds, and then put the broom back, and this is the moment, this is the hinge of the entire night: a sixty-nine-year-old man in a dressing gown put a broom back in a cupboard because he could not do it.

He went and looked at it again from a meter away, crouched, with his knees complaining.

He said, in German, to a seabird: "Also. Was machen wir mit dir."

Then he went and got a wine case.

It was one of six left from a delivery in 2025, and it was the good kind, wood, with the dividers still in it, and he took the dividers out and knelt on his own terrace in a dressing gown at ten at night and lined the bottom of it with a cardigan.

The cardigan was cashmere and had cost, in the old money, something he would have told you if you had asked.

Getting the bird into the case took eleven minutes and he was bitten twice, hard enough to break the skin on the web of his thumb, and he did not swear, and he did not stop, and when it was finally in he crouched over the open case with his hands shaking slightly and said, again, to a bird, in the language he keeps for himself:

"So. Ja. Gut. Ganz ruhig."

Then he carried it inside, holding it flat and level in front of him with both hands, the way you carry soup, and he set it down on the floor of the big room by the wall to the left of the glass, which is where the light does not reach and where it would be quiet, and where, for nine years, there used to be a dog bed.

He did not think about the dog bed. He has not thought about the dog bed in a decade.

❦

By eleven he had established the following: he did not know what it ate, whether it should have water, whether it was injured, whether it would die in the night, or what a person was supposed to do with it in the morning.

He knew one other thing, which he had known for thirteen years and had never once found intolerable until that moment.

There was nobody to ask.

You have to understand what the mesh is to a man like Klaus. It is not that he could not have one. Príngales has been going since 2032 and the nodes are made out of tin cans and salvage by people who wear their trousers wrong, and any one of forty people would have come up his road and put one in for the asking, at no charge, out of the specific pleasure of doing a favor for a man who has never done one.

That is exactly the problem. He would have had to ask, and then he would have been a man with a mesh unit, on a network run by hippies, in a village conversation he did not join and could not leave. He has spent thirteen years explaining to Sonja, in the reasonable voice, that the entire thing is a toy, that it will not survive contact with a real economy, and that when the connections come back ⁓ wenn alles zurückkommt ⁓ nobody will remember any of it.

At twenty past eleven he stood in his own kitchen with a bird in a wine case eight meters away and said, to his wife, in a voice he did not entirely recognize:

"Go down to the Mendozas and ask them what to do with it."

❦

Sonja put her coat on and walked down the hill in the dark.

It is a kilometer and a half to the Mendoza place, which has a node in a shed, and she was gone fifty minutes, and Klaus sat on a hard chair in the big room where he could see the case and did not go near it, because he had decided that quiet was the thing it needed and quiet was a thing he could provide.

She came back with a piece of paper in her hand and a second piece of information in her face.

The paper said: dark, quiet, do not feed it, do not give it water, do not touch it more than you must.

"And?" said Klaus.

"And they say ask the onion sisters."

"Ask the what?"

"Die Zwiebelschwestern," said Sonja, in German, because she has been married to him for eighteen years and knows exactly which language a thing has to be in for him to hear it. "Up above Querencia. In the north. Three women. They ran the campaign for twenty-five years."

"What campaign?"

Sonja looked at him.

"For the birds," she said.

❦

The night that followed was, in Klaus Reiter's own private accounting, the single most frustrating night of the entire collapse, and this includes 2029.

Because here is how you have a conversation on this island at midnight when you are the last man on the coast without a node.

You write your question on a piece of paper. Your wife walks a kilometer and a half down a dark hill and gives it to a family who are already in bed and who get up anyway. They type it. It goes north over the ridges, node to node, and lands in a kitchen above Querencia where somebody is awake because somebody up there is always awake. An answer comes back. And then your wife walks a kilometer and a half up the dark hill to your door and hands you four sentences.

And then you have a follow-up question.

Sonja made that walk three times.

The third time she came back at ten past two in the morning, and she was not angry, which was worse, and she put the paper on the table and sat down and took her shoes off and said nothing at all.

The paper said: is it standing? is the wing dropped or held? has it been sick? do not answer tonight if it means she walks again.

Klaus read that last part twice.

He looked at his wife's feet.

He went and got the laptop.

❦

It is in the second drawer of the console in the hall, under the tablecloths, and Sonja did not have to be asked where it was and did not comment when he came back with it.

He bought it in 2026, in Düsseldorf, in a shop, with a card, for a sixteen-year-old who had been on this island for eleven weeks and had spoken perhaps forty sentences in that time. It was for schoolwork. It was expensive. He remembers choosing it and he remembers being pleased with himself, and he does not remember whether it was ever used, and it has been in that drawer since the autumn of 2033.

The battery had been dead for nine years. It came up on mains at twenty to three in the morning and asked him for a password in a language he had set, and he got it in two.

Then he sat there with a laptop open in front of him at three in the morning in a dark house with a seabird in a wine case, and understood, with total clarity and no feeling whatsoever, that he was going to have to go down that hill in the morning and ask a family who wear their trousers wrong to come and put a tin can on his roof.

❦

They came at eleven. There were three of them, and the youngest was about fourteen, and they were up and down off his roof in an hour and would not take anything for it, and the eldest walked the whole terrace afterward on the pretext of checking the panel angle and looked at Klaus's view for a long time with an expression of enormous professional interest.

The node is a Pringles can. He has looked at it twice since and it is still a Pringles can.

At twelve minutes past twelve, Klaus Reiter's household joined Príngales, and eleven seconds after that, a stranger four valleys away typed buena fog, vecino onto a screen in his hall, and Klaus stared at it for some time before he worked out that it was not a mistake.

By half past twelve he was talking to the north.

❦

The onion sisters, it turns out, type like three separate people, which is because they are.

The first voice was flat and gave instructions in imperatives and asked exactly the questions on the paper and two more. Is the bird standing on both feet or favoring. Does the wing sit in or hang. Has it brought anything up. When he answered both, in, no, it typed: then there is nothing wrong with it except geography, and Klaus, alone in his hall, said "what?" out loud to a screen.

The second voice was enormous and asked whether the case had a lid on it and, when he said no, typed a paragraph and a half about why it needed one that he found himself reading twice.

The third voice typed, at four minutes past one:

it will go tonight if you give it wind and a drop and dark. no lights. not one.

He typed: what do you mean, not one.

There was a pause of about ninety seconds.

not one, the third voice typed again, and said nothing else for the rest of the night.

❦

He drove it down at dusk.

The Fiat is solar and decrepit and the passenger mirror has been chewed off by a goat, and he took the coast road with the wine case on the seat beside him with a towel over it, and he drove, for the first time in some years, extremely carefully.

He went past the turning for the Bienestar. He did not look at it.

There is a place beyond El Puertito where the road runs close to a low black shelf of rock and you can get down to the water in about forty meters if your knees are prepared to discuss it. He parked. He carried the case down over the rocks in the last of the light, holding it flat and level, the way you carry soup.

Then he took the towel off and opened the lid and stepped back the way the flat voice had told him to.

Nothing happened for about a minute and a half.

Then the bird came up out of the wine case onto the black rock, and stood there, and the wind off the water got in under it and lifted every feather it had so that it doubled in size, and Klaus Reiter watched a thing that had been a heap in a box for twenty hours turn into something else entirely in about four seconds.

It ran. It was ugly for six steps and then the wind took the weight, and it was off the rock and out low over the water, and it did not go up so much as it went out, fast, and level, and gone.

He stood on the rocks for a while afterward in the dark with an empty box.

He is not a man who has words for what that was, and he did not look for any. What he thought about, driving home, in the way a man thinks about a thing that has moved him and that he has no shelf for, was that it had weighed nothing at all.

Nothing. He had carried it twenty meters over rocks and it had been like carrying an empty coat.

He thought about that the whole way up the hill, and then he put the wine case in the garage, and then he went in and had a whiskey, which he does perhaps twice a year.

❦

That is the good part. That is where it would end if this were a story about a man changing.

❦

The node stayed on the roof.

There was no discussion about taking it down. There was, for about four days, no discussion about it at all, and Sonja watched her husband walk past the console table in the hall six or seven times a day and not sit down at it, and said nothing, because eighteen years.

On the fifth day he sat down.

He read for two hours. He read the whole board back as far as it went, which on Príngales is about eleven days before the older stuff rolls off, and what he found was: a man in Guía looking for a particular bearing size, three people arguing about whether to move a market day, a woman posting the weather off the north ridge every morning at six with no commentary, somebody's lost goat, a long and detailed exchange about pump seals, and forty or fifty messages that were simply people telling each other things that had happened.

He found it, and this is not a joke and he would say it out loud, unstructured.

On the sixth day he posted.

❦

His first message to the island was two hundred and forty words long and it began:

To the users of this network. My name is Klaus Reiter and I am the owner of the land at Los Menores and of the headland above Diego Hernández.

It went on to observe that the network appeared to have no register of members, no agreed protocol for the resolution of disputes, and no standard format for the posting of commercial offers, and that in his professional experience ⁓ he gave the years ⁓ an unstructured system of this kind becomes unusable at a certain scale and that the time to address it is before, not after. He offered to draft a framework. He noted that he had drafted several.

He read it over twice and was pleased with it, which he had not been about anything he had written in some years, and he sent it, and then he sat back in the chair in the hall.

Nobody was rude to him.

That is what he could not get past afterward. Not one person was rude. A man in Icod thanked him for the offer. The woman who posts the weather did not respond. Somebody asked, without any edge in it at all, whether he had a bearing in the size the man in Guía wanted. Two people welcomed him to the network. And then, over the following hour, the board went on doing exactly what it had been doing before he arrived, which was pump seals and a lost goat and people telling each other things that had happened.

His framework was never mentioned again by anybody.

He posted twice more that week.

❦

He found it on the ninth night.

He was reading backward through a long thread about a market at Alcalá, because he had decided to compile a list of the regular traders and their goods, which nobody had asked him for and which he had got a surprising amount of satisfaction out of, and somewhere in the middle of it two people started talking about the entertainment.

There had been players at the market. There was a bit with a rope and a bit with a dog. And there was a new figure this year that everybody was very taken with, and one of them was describing it to the other, who had missed it.

He is tall, she wrote. He is extremely tall, it is somebody on stilts under the coat, and he has a stick, and he has a paper he carries in front of him like this, and every time anybody tries to give him anything, food or a drink or a chair, he reads them the paper instead.

And he never says anything, she wrote. He never says one word the whole time. He just points at the paper and then at the thing and then at the paper.

And everybody knows him, she wrote. Everybody knows who he is. You do not have to explain it to anybody. And when he goes off at the end he walks backwards all the way, still reading, and it kills them every time, it absolutely kills them.

la noche del vecino, the other one wrote back. we had him at the fiesta in june.

that's the one.

Klaus Reiter sat in his hall in front of a laptop he had bought in Düsseldorf in 2026 for a child who did not want it, and read a description of himself performed for laughs at a market in Alcalá, on a network he had joined six days earlier, at eleven o'clock at night, alone.

He read it four times.

He did not reply. There was, in fairness to him, nothing to reply to; nobody had said his name.

After a while he closed the laptop and put it back in the second drawer of the console under the tablecloths, and then took it out again and left it on the table, and then went to bed.

❦

Sonja found him on the terrace at seven the next morning, which is where he is at seven every morning, with the coffee and the notebook.

The October entry was already written. She had seen it going in the day before: Q4 2049.

She sat down opposite him with her own cup, which she does perhaps once a month, and they looked out at it together for a while ⁓ the dead golf course brown-green, the shell of the Bienestar on the headland with the fire scars on the concrete, the water beyond it going silver, and the whole coast below them lying there in the early light with everything on it switched off.

"It went, then," she said. "The bird."

"It went."

"Mm." She drank her coffee. "Where do they go?"

"South," said Klaus. "That is what they said. All the way south, and then they come back."

Sonja nodded slowly.

She looked down the hill for a while at the lights that had been on all night on the terrace and along the drop and out over the deck, nineteen of them, that had been on every night for twenty-two years, over the brightest thing on that whole dark coast.

"It is a lot of light," she said.

"It is a security matter," said Klaus.

"Mm," said Sonja.

And she did not say the rest of it, because she is not certain, and because she would not know how to begin, and because in eighteen years she has learned precisely what happens to a sentence like that in this house.

They finished their coffee. The sun came up over the ridge behind them and put the villa's shadow out across the sand rectangle where a pool was never poured.

That night, and every night after it, the lights came on.

A few words for the curious

  • Sturmvogel: German, storm bird. It is the family name for the whole tribe of tube-nosed seabirds ⁓ the petrels and the shearwaters ⁓ and it is a better name than any of the words we use in English, because it says the thing about them that matters: they are made for the weather that everybody else is made to shelter from.
  • Príngales: the island's mesh network, running since 2032 on salvage, solar, and Pringles cans. It has no owner, no register, no protocol for the resolution of disputes, and no standard format for commercial offers. It has worked continuously for ten years.
  • The wine case: six left of a delivery from 2025. Good wood, removable dividers. It has now been used once for its second-best purpose and the man who owns it has kept it.
  • What she nearly said: nothing. She nearly said nothing. She is not an ornithologist, she has no data, she has never once been told the figure for that municipality, and she could not have defended the thought if she had spoken it. All she had was a coastline in the dark with one lit thing on it and a bird that had come down out of the sky onto exactly that.
