Episode 27

Torbellino

The north coast road, and a plaza in La Guancha. One ordinary Thursday in March, 2042.

Welcome back to Nivaria. Slow news from a quiet island. This is Caitlyn Meeks. Thanks for being here.

He wakes before anybody, the way he has woken before anybody for eighteen years, and he does the first job.

The first job is the count.

It is not a thought. Nothing he does is a thought in the way you mean it. It is simply that a nose in a room full of sleeping people goes down the row the way your eye goes down a shelf, and the row this morning is: the tall one who smells of woodsmoke and kerosene and the sharp green of the stuff she puts on the wicks, and next to her the small loud one who smells of milk and river stones, and past them the big one who smells of solder and coffee, and past him the two who smell almost the same as each other and always have, and then the one who is new and still smells faintly of somewhere else, and then the doorway.

Everyone is here.

He puts his head back down.

That is the first job and he has never once been thanked for it and does not require it, because it is not a favor. A count is just a thing you do before you can rest properly, the way you turn around three times before you lie down, for reasons nobody in the long history of dogs has ever been able to explain and every single one of them has understood.

He sleeps again for a while. He sleeps more than he used to. Nobody has said anything about that either.

❦

Here is the world in the order that it exists.

First is smell, and smell is not a sense the way you have senses; it is the room. It is where the room is. It is what happened in the room before you came in and who has crossed it since, and it does not go away when you stop paying attention, it just goes quiet, like a light left on in a house you are not in.

Second is sound, and sound is mostly people, and people are mostly two things: the sound they make and the thing they mean by it. He has about forty of these. Torbe means turn your head. Vamos means the day is starting and you will be lifted. Espera means a hand is about to be needed somewhere and it is not your business yet. He knows the sound of his own name in six mouths and they are six different sounds and they all mean him.

Third, distantly, is seeing, which is useful for catching things and for finding the edge of a table, and which he does not trust.

Fourth is the ground under him, which tells him where the island is going.

Everything else is weather.

❦

The morning goes the way mornings go.

He is lifted down out of the truck bed rather than jumping it, and this is not remarked on by anybody, including him. The hands that do it are the tall one's, and they go under his chest and behind and take his whole weight at once, which is a specific way of picking up a dog and there are only three people alive who do it correctly and she is the best of them.

He does his round of the camp. There is a wheel that was not there yesterday and has been somewhere with fish. There is a bag that a person from the next valley touched. There is, at the edge of the ground, in the dew, the whole track of a cat's night written out in full, and he reads all of it and finds it uninteresting and thorough.

Then he stands still for four minutes while the tall one puts the coat on him.

He does not like the coat. He has never liked the coat. It has small hard shoulders on it and it goes over his head and the collar of it is stiff and ridiculous and lifts his ruff into a shape that is not a shape a neck should be. He submits to it entirely, without a sound, every single time, because the coat means the rest of the day, and he has understood the arrangement since he was small: you put up with the collar and then there is the plaza and the noise and the enormous warm animal that a crowd makes, and then, at the end, there is the fan of cards and the thing that is his.

She fixes the last strap and puts her forehead on his forehead for one second.

"Vamos," she says.

❦

The road smells like the sea for an hour and then like pine and then like the sea again.

He rides the way he has ridden for nine years, which is inside the bag on her back with the top open and his head out of it, at the exact height of a person's face, watching the island come apart behind them. The bag rides higher than it used to. She carries it higher because her shoulders have got stronger and because there is less of him than there was, and neither of these facts has been said out loud in the whole of the last two years.

At some point the road passes a place where a thing died a long time ago and he lifts his nose and gets the whole story of it in one breath, and puts his head back down.

They stop at the top of a long descent where there is water for the truck. The small loud one gets out and runs a circle around the whole vehicle for no reason. He watches this with the tolerance of a very old man watching a very young one do something with a ball.

And on the wall at the edge of the stopping place, in the wind, there is a feather.

❦

He gets it in his mouth before anyone can say his name in the tone that means no.

It is gray. It is long and stiff along one edge and soft at the base, and it is not from a bird that walks about in a yard. It has been in salt water and dried in salt air and it is nothing whatsoever like a hen or a pigeon or the little brown things in the bushes.

It is sea.

And the smell of it goes down into him and opens a room he has not been in for a long time.

❦

The room is a floor, and the floor is stone, and the stone is cold in a particular way that no other floor has ever been cold, because it is polished and it is enormous and it goes all the way to a wall of glass.

He was small in that room. He is not clear on how small. There is a shape to it, though: things were high up. The place where the food went was high up. The hands came down from a long way.

He had a bed there. It was by the wall on the left of the glass, and it was his, and it smelled of him more than anything has ever smelled of him.

And on the other side of the glass was the terrace, and the terrace had lights.

He does not have a word for what the lights were. He has the feeling of the lights, which is: white, and always, and hot near the ground, and enough to make the terrace a place where it was never night. He used to lie on the warm stone under them with his chin on his feet, and there were moths, and the moths were a job.

And then, in a certain season, the sky started dropping birds on it.

❦

You want to understand what this was like from where he was standing.

There is a night. It is warm, and windy, and the lights are on the way they are always on, and something comes down out of the black above the glare fast and wrong and hits the stone about four meters from him with a sound like a wet coat falling off a hook.

And then it is there. On the terrace. A thing the size of him, from the sky, on the ground, alive.

He was, then, a dog of about seven months, of a breed developed over four hundred years to sit on the laps of people in cold countries, and he had never been told anything about anything.

He did the correct thing.

He did the thing that is under everything in him, under four hundred years of laps, all the way at the bottom where the old wolf keeps its few remaining possessions: he went and got it, and he did not bite down, and he carried it in.

He was so proud that he could not walk straight.

❦

The tall man was at the glass table with the small light and the papers.

Torbe came in with the bird in his mouth and his whole back end going and put it down on the floor by the man's shoe, which is the correct place, which is the place, and stood over it, and looked up, and waited to be told.

The man said a word. It was short and it went down at the end.

He did not look at the bird.

That is the part. That is the only part. He looked at the dog, and he made the short word, and then he made a longer sound toward the door, and a woman came and there was a noise of a cloth and the bird was taken out of the room and the man went back to the papers, and at no moment in any of it did his eyes go to the floor by his shoe.

Torbe stayed standing over the empty place for a while, because the job was not finished, because nobody had said the thing you say.

Then he went out through the flap and lay down under the lights.

❦

He brought four more that autumn.

He brought eleven the next year, and nine the year after that, and some years the sky gave up more than others and he has no idea why and never did. He got better at it. He learned that they were easier if you got them before they got themselves under the plant pots. He learned the exact carry that does not hurt them, which is high at the shoulders and not tight, and which he worked out himself, over about three birds, at eight months old, because a mouth is a hand that can feel what it is holding.

He was, in the terms that were available to him, extremely good at this.

Nobody ever took one.

It went the same way every time. The short word going down at the end. The eyes on him and not on it. The cloth, the woman, the door. And afterward the thing that dogs do, which is check whether they have got the job wrong ⁓ do it again, do it more clearly, put it closer, look up longer.

He did that for four autumns.

And then he stopped.

There is no drama in the stopping and there was no decision in it, and this is the honest way to say what happened: the behavior was not reinforced, and behaviors that are not reinforced go out like a fire nobody is feeding. In the fifth autumn he heard one come down on the far side of the terrace and he lifted his head and he did not get up.

He is a dog. He does not have the sentence for this. He never had the sentence and he never will.

But somewhere in there is a small worn place, the way a rope wears where it has run over the same stone for years, and if you had put your hand on that dog in the fifth autumn and asked him, with your hand, whether he had been good, he would not have known what to tell you.

❦

He came out of the room and back onto the wall in the wind, in March, in 2042, with a feather in his mouth.

The tall one was crouched in front of him with her hand out.

She did not say the sound that means no. She waited. And after a moment he put the feather into her palm the way he has put a hundred things into that palm, and she looked at it properly, turning it over, the barbs and the shaft and the salt-dulled gray of it, for four or five seconds. Which is a long time to look at a feather.

"Pardela," she said.

Then she said something else, longer, to the big one at the truck, and the big one came and looked too, and the two of them stood there in the wind over a feather in a hand and were interested in it.

And then she gave it back.

He carried it the rest of the way to La Guancha in the bag with his head out, holding it very carefully in the side of his mouth, and did not eat it, and did not lose it, and put it down on the flagstones at the edge of the plaza when they arrived because there was a great deal to smell.

❦

The other thing in the room, which he does not visit, and which comes up sideways sometimes when a stranger's hands smell a particular way:

A man came to the villa once, at the end, when the food had gone strange and the lights had stopped being always. He smelled of animals and of not washing and of something else that had no name and was the reason Torbe went under the low table and stayed there.

The tall man's hands and the visiting man's hands, and a sound between them going back and forth, and Torbe under the table with his chin on the cold and his eyes on the shoes.

And then the young one came.

He knew her already. She had been in the house sometimes, tall and quiet and not staying, and she smelled of outside and of smoke and of something the house did not have. She came in that day and she stood still for a moment in the doorway, and Torbe felt her go still, the way you feel weather change through a floor.

And then hands came under the low table, and they came in the correct way, under the chest and behind, and he was up against a jacket and moving, and the jacket smelled of fire and salt and the outside, and there was a bag, and he went into it, and the top stayed open.

He did not look back at the room. There is no version of this where a dog looks back at a room.

He remembers the going, though. He remembers the going very well: a great deal of walking, downhill, fast, in the dark, and a stopping at a car and a noise of something being done to it that took a while, and the smell of the young one's anger which is a completely different smell from the smell of fear, and then more downhill, and then the sea got closer and closer until it was the whole world, and then there was a cave.

❦

The cave had eighty smells in it and he learned all of them in one evening.

There was fire, and old smoke gone into rock, and paint, and sausage fat, and beer in wood, and about two hundred people who had been in that room over the years and left a little of themselves in it. It was the richest room he had ever stood in. Compared to the polished floor it was like being able to hear after a life of a hand over your ear.

And on the second night, or the third, a big man crouched down in front of him, a man who smelled of lime and dog and cave-damp, and said a sound.

It was a new sound. It was longer than the old one and it went up in the middle.

He did not answer it.

They said it again the next day, and the day after, and by the end of the week the sound had begun to arrive with hands and with food and with the tall young one turning round to look for him when she said it, and something in him that had been holding its breath for nine years put it down.

He answered to it on a Thursday.

He has answered to it ever since, and he does not remember the other one, and this is not sad. It is simply how a dog's name works. A name is not a fact about you. A name is the sound that gets you looked at, and when the looking changes, the sound should change too.

❦

La Guancha, at four in the afternoon, before a show, is the best place in the world.

He works the plaza. This is his own arrangement and nobody assigns it. He goes along the front of the crowd at knee height with the coat on and the ridiculous collar and lets people put their hands on him, and he takes a full breath of each of them as he goes, and he is not being petted. He is reading.

He gets: the fish family, all six of them, the same smell in different strengths. He gets a woman who has been crying and stopped. He gets a boy with a lizard in his pocket, which he notes and files and does nothing about. He gets somebody who is frightened of him, whom he moves past politely and does not look at, because he was frightened once by a big hand and he has never once made another creature feel it. He gets an old man whose legs smell of medicine and whose hands, when they come down, are so gentle that Torbe stops and stands there for a whole minute and lets him.

By the time the light goes gold he has the whole plaza in his head, and he goes and lies in the shade under the folding stage and waits, with his chin on his feet, breathing it and sorting it, the way you might go through a bag of nails you have just been given.

He is not resting. He is working.

He is deciding who it is going to be.

❦

Here is the act, from the inside, and here is the thing that not one person in La Tropa has ever known in nine years.

They think it is chance. They think he takes a card and picks a person the way a coin picks a side, and they have built a whole beloved piece of theater on top of a dog making an arbitrary decision in front of two hundred people, and every single one of them ⁓ the tall one included, who loves him and lifts him correctly and has done this act with him four hundred times ⁓ believes that the wonderful thing about it is that it means nothing.

It means something.

He goes to the one who smells wrong.

Not wrong like bad. Wrong like a room with a window open in it: the salt-and-metal smell that comes up off a person who is frightened, or sick, or grieving, or holding something they have not put down. It is as plain to him as a shout. It has been plain to him since he was a puppy on a cold floor in a house where one person smelled like that all the time and the other one did not.

So he goes along the fan of cards with the gravity of a customs inspection, which is the tall one's joke about him and is also completely accurate, because he is taking his time on purpose. And he takes one in his teeth. And then he goes down off the stage and along the front of that crowd, past every reaching hand, past the children calling him, past the fish family and the boy with the lizard, and he stops in front of the one he chose three hours ago under the stage, and he puts the card at their feet.

Tonight it is the old man with the medicine legs and the gentle hands.

The crowd makes the noise. The old man's hands are not steady. He gets the card up and he turns it over and he reads it out loud, because that is the law on this island and everybody knows it: El Sol.

Two hundred people make a sound like a wave.

Torbe is already going back up the ramp, because the job is done and there is a second half, and behind him a man of eighty is being clapped on the back by his neighbors and is doing something complicated with his face.

Nobody will ever tell that old man that he was chosen. Nobody in that plaza knows that he was chosen. The dog is not able to explain it and would not if he could, and the tall one will say, afterward, to somebody who asks, that he picks them at random and that this is the beauty of it.

He has been finding the person who needs to be found, in every town on this island, twice a week, for nine years.

Nobody has ever noticed.

❦

There was a night in the autumn ⁓ he does not have autumn, he has the season when the wind turns and the food changes ⁓ when he smelled the villa on a man in a car park.

Not a memory of it. The actual smell, walking about on a person: the ironing, the cold stone, the particular coffee, the soap. It came across a concrete deck full of firelight and people and went into him like a nail.

He got up. He is seventeen and he got up fast.

He went across that deck and stood in front of the tall man and looked directly up at him, which he does not do, and he said one thing. It was short and it went down at the end, and it was, although nobody there could have known this and he certainly did not, the same shape as a word he had heard on a stone floor a long time ago.

Then he turned around and walked away and did not look back at him.

The man looked at the blue dog and did not know him.

Torbe went and lay down under the tall one's chair and put his chin on her boot, and she reached down without looking and put her hand on his head, and that was all of it, and he has not thought about it since, because a dog does not think about a thing like that afterward. He simply arrived, in that moment, with everything he had, and then it was over, and there was a fire, and he was warm.

❦

The second half runs long. They always do.

He does his other bit, the launch, which is the thing where he comes out of the bag over the tall one's shoulder in the middle of the fire and lands running, and the crowd loses its mind every time.

He is very tired afterward. He is tired in a way that gets into his back legs, and there is a stair down off the stage that he takes carefully now, one at a time, and at the bottom of it the tall one is standing without appearing to be standing there.

She does not pick him up in front of the crowd. She has never once done that. She waits until the fire is out and the plaza is emptying and the crates are going onto the truck, and then she comes and crouches and takes his coat off, and the collar comes over his head and his ruff goes back to the shape a neck should be, and she puts both hands under him, chest and behind, and lifts him up and puts him in the bag with the top open.

"Bueno," she says. "Ya está."

❦

The fire that night is a small one, behind the truck, with the crates for seats.

The whole row is there. The tall one, and the loud small one who is asleep against her, and the big one with his hands round a cup, and the two who smell nearly the same, and the new one who is starting, very slowly, to smell like the rest of them, which he has noticed and approves of.

He goes round them all once. He gets a hand from each without asking for one, which is the correct arrangement and has taken nine years to establish. Then he lies down at the edge where the heat stops, because he is old and the heat is too much for him now in a way it never used to be, and he puts his chin on his feet.

The feather is somewhere in the bag. He knows exactly where.

Above the plaza the sky is doing the thing it does, which is nothing much, and the wind comes off the water smelling of the whole enormous cold outside, and somewhere a long way down there are birds on it that he will never see and could not have helped anyway.

He does the count. Everyone is here.

He sleeps.

A few words for the curious

  • Torbellino: whirlwind. He was given the name in a cave at nine years old, having had a different one for his entire life until then, and he learned it in about a week, because a name is not a fact about a dog. It is the sound that gets him looked at.
  • The count: dogs in a group check the group. They do it on waking, they do it before settling, and they do it without being asked or thanked, and if one of you is missing they will tell you about it in whatever way they have. There is no mystery here and it is not devotion. It is arithmetic, done by nose, by somebody for whom the number matters.
  • The carry: a soft-mouthed dog can hold a living bird without hurting it, and can work out how in about three attempts, because a mouth that can feel what it is holding is a hand. There is no training in this story. There was no one to train him.
  • What the act actually is: not disclosed to La Tropa, and this is not a secret he is keeping, because keeping a secret requires knowing that other people don't have it. He simply goes to the person who smells the way a person smells when something is wrong. Dogs do this. They have always done this. We have known about it for as long as we have had dogs and we have never once managed to be unimpressed by it.
  • Pomeranian: bred small over four centuries from a large northern sledge dog, for the specific purpose of being carried about by people with nothing to do. Under all of that, unmodified and entirely intact, is a dog who will go out into the dark and bring you back the thing that fell, over and over, for four years, on the chance that this time you will look at it.
